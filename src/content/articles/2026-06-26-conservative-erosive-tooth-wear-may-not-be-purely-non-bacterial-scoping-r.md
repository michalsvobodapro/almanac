summary: A scoping review in the Journal of Dentistry challenges the textbook framing
  of erosive tooth wear (ETW) as a purely chemical, bacteria-independent process.
  Seven eligible studies suggest the oral biofilm modulates enamel erosion — both
  protectively and pathologically — through pellicle structure, bacterial adhesion,
  and taxon-level dysbiosis. The evidence is heterogeneous and preliminary, but the
  conceptual shift has real diagnostic implications.
summaryDeep: 'Erosive tooth wear is taught as the one major dental hard-tissue condition
  where bacteria are irrelevant — acid from diet or reflux does the damage, full stop.
  This scoping review, following PRISMA-ScR, complicates that picture. From 2,355
  records, only 7 studies met inclusion criteria, reflecting how nascent this research
  area is. Two studies show biofilm provides partial enamel protection against erosive
  challenge; two others demonstrate that bioactive compounds alter pellicle structure
  and reduce erosion; three directly probe microbial ecology with inconsistent but
  suggestive findings. The heterogeneity is substantial — in vitro, in situ, and clinical
  designs are all represented — so no quantitative synthesis is possible. What the
  review does establish is a plausible biological mechanism: the biofilm and its metabolic
  products interact with the acquired pellicle in ways that either buffer or amplify
  acid attack. For conservative dentistry students, this is a conceptual update worth
  filing: future ETW management may involve microbiome-targeted adjuncts alongside
  dietary counselling and fluoride.'
