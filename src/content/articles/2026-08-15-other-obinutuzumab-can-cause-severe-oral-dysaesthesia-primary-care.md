excerptFull: Oral dysaesthesia is a chronic pain condition which can significantly
  impact patients' quality of life. Due to the multifactorial aetiology of the condition,
  it can be challenging for both primary and secondary care clinicians to identify
  and treat all possible causative factors. Comprehensive history taking and focussed
  chairside examination are imperative in the determination of predisposing factors
  for oral dysaesthesia. Immunotherapy is a concept in cancer therapy which is becoming
  the standard of care in the management of certain malignancies. Patients may be
  well enough to attend general dental appointments while on immunotherapy treatment
  and therefore increasingly primary care clinicians can expect this cohort of patients
  under their care. Obinutuzumab is a monoclonal antibody which can be used as a first-line
  therapy in subsets of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. We present our experience of a patient
  who suffered severe, therapy-limiting oral dysaesthesia, while undergoing treatment
  with obinutuzumab for follicular lymphoma. We present the clinical findings and
  investigations undertaken, which lead to a diagnosis of oral dysaesthesia in this
  case, and discuss the evidence base surrounding immunotherapy associated with oral
  dysaesthesia. To the authors' knowledge this is the first reported case of oral
  dysaesthesia associated with obinutuzumab therapy, but as more patients are treated
  with novel immunotherapy agents, this may be an important treatment side effect
  to highlight for future research and dental clinicians' knowledge.

summaryDeep: 'Obinutuzumab is a CD20-targeting monoclonal antibody used as first-line
  therapy in follicular lymphoma. This case describes a patient who developed severe,
  therapy-limiting oral dysaesthesia during treatment — a chronic pain condition characterised
  by burning, tingling, or altered sensation without identifiable mucosal pathology.
  The diagnostic workup required exclusion of candidiasis, xerostomia, nutritional
  deficiencies, and neuropathic causes before the drug was implicated. The authors
  note this is the first published case linking obinutuzumab to oral dysaesthesia,
  and they anticipate more as immunotherapy use expands. For a dental student, the
  clinical lesson is twofold: always take a full medication history including biologics
  and immunotherapy agents, and be prepared to recognise oral dysaesthesia as a diagnosis
  of exclusion that may require multidisciplinary communication with the patient''s
  oncology team.'
