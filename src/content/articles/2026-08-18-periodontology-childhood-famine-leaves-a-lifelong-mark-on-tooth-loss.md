summaryDeep: 'Early-life nutritional deprivation has long been suspected to compromise
  dental development and long-term oral health, but causal inference is difficult
  when famine exposure correlates with poverty, poor healthcare access, and other
  confounders. This study exploits historical famines — events that affected entire
  birth cohorts regardless of individual socioeconomic status — as exogenous shocks,
  pooling data from 10 population-based studies across three continents. The finding
  that famine-exposed cohorts show higher rates of late-life edentulism, even after
  adjustment, points toward a developmental programming effect on tooth and periodontal
  tissue quality. The mechanism is likely multifactorial: enamel hypoplasia, impaired
  immune development, and reduced bone mineral density during critical growth windows.
  For clinicians, this is a reminder that edentulism in older patients may reflect
  life-course exposures far beyond adult oral hygiene habits — relevant context for
  treatment planning and patient communication.'
