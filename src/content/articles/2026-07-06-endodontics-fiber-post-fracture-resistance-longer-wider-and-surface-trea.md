excerpt: 'Fiber-reinforced posts are widely used to restore endodontically treated
  anterior teeth, yet the relative contributions of post length, diameter, material,
  and surface treatment to fracture resistance in maxillary lateral incisors remain
  unclear. To determine the individual and interactive effects of fiber post length,
  diameter, material, and surface treatment on the fracture resistance and failure
  modes of endodontically treated maxillary lateral incisors restored with composite
  cores and monolithic zirconia crowns. In a controlled laboratory study (2 × 2 ×
  2 × 2 design; 16 groups; n = 10/group; N = 160), extracted maxillary lateral incisors
  received standardized endodontic treatment, fiber post placement, composite core
  build-ups, and monolithic 5Y-PSZ zirconia crowns. Factors were: post length (8 vs.
  12 mm), diameter (0.9 vs. 1.1 mm), post material/system (tested glass-fiber post
  system vs. tested quartz-fiber post system), and surface treatment (ethanol-cleaned
  vs. airborne-particle abrasion + silane). A uniform 2-mm ferrule, PDL simulation,
  and epoxy embedding were used. Specimens were thermocycled (10,000 cycles, 5–55

summaryDeep: 'Restoring endodontically treated maxillary lateral incisors with fiber
  posts is a common clinical scenario, yet the relative contribution of post geometry
  and surface treatment has rarely been tested in a fully factorial design. This 2×2×2×2
  in-vitro study (16 groups, n=10 each, N=160) used standardized endodontic treatment,
  composite core build-ups, and monolithic 5Y-PSZ zirconia crowns with a uniform 2
  mm ferrule. Thermocycling (10,000 cycles) preceded 135° loading to failure. The
  main effects were additive: 12 mm length added ~86 N over 8 mm, 1.1 mm diameter
  outperformed 0.9 mm, and airborne-particle abrasion plus silane surface treatment
  outperformed ethanol cleaning alone. Post material (glass-fiber vs. quartz-fiber)
  showed smaller differences. Failure mode analysis is critical — repairable vs. non-repairable
  fractures — and the study provides χ² data on this. The in-vitro design limits direct
  clinical translation, but the additive effect model gives a clear hierarchy: maximize
  post length within the available root, use the larger diameter where anatomy allows,
  and never skip surface treatment.'
