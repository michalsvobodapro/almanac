excerptFull: Aim To evaluate three clinical techniques (current practice, Silhouette
  with a flow rate protocol, and eAdvantage) for inhalation sedation. Methods This
  study was conducted at the Eastman Dental Hospital (London, UK) using quality improvement
  methodology. The three clinical techniques were used over a six-week period. Data
  were collected on the volume of nitrous oxide (N2O) gas used per patient, success
  rate, as well as patient and staff feedback. Results Ninety-eight episodes of inhalation
  sedation were observed during the pilot period. Both Silhouette and eAdvantage techniques
  demonstrated impressive reductions in the volume of N2O used per patient (58% and
  70%, respectively) when compared to current practice. The success rate was 97% for
  current practice and eAdvantage, and 86% for Silhouette. Conclusion Current practice
  for inhalation sedation relies on a flow rate that is set by the clinical team,
  with the patient breathing from a reservoir of gas. This technique is potentially
  over-delivering gas, and new techniques and equipment that reduce or eliminate this
  free-flow rate show significant environmental benefits.

summaryDeep: 'Ninety-eight inhalation sedation episodes were observed over six weeks
  at Eastman Dental Hospital using quality improvement methodology. Three techniques
  were compared: conventional free-flow (current practice), Silhouette with a flow-rate
  protocol, and eAdvantage (on-demand gas release). Both alternatives dramatically
  reduced N₂O volume per patient — Silhouette by 58%, eAdvantage by 70% — compared
  to free-flow. Success rates were 97% for both current practice and eAdvantage, but
  dropped to 86% for Silhouette, suggesting the flow-rate protocol compromises clinical
  reliability. Nitrous oxide is a potent greenhouse gas, and dentistry''s contribution
  to N₂O emissions is a growing regulatory and ethical concern in the EU and UK. The
  eAdvantage result is particularly compelling: equivalent clinical success with 70%
  less gas. This is a study design (quality improvement, n=98) that limits causal
  inference, but the effect sizes are large enough to be practice-relevant. For students,
  this is a preview of the sustainability pressures that will shape clinical protocols
