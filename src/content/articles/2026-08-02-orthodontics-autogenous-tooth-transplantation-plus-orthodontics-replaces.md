excerpt: Objectives This case report describes orthodontics-assisted autogenous tooth
  transplantation for a maxillary incisor injury in a pediatric patient with crowding
  and Class II malocclusion. Material and methods A 10-year-old boy presented with
  a compromised maxillary left central incisor with a periapical lesion after failed
  endodontic treatment, together with bimaxillary crowding and Class II malocclusion.
  The mandibular right second premolar was transplanted to replace the maxillary incisor
  and was integrated with comprehensive orthodontic treatment. Results After transplantation,
  endodontic treatment was completed when pulp vitality could not be maintained. During
  long-term follow-up, the transplanted tooth remained clinically stable, with healthy
  periodontal tissues and no progressive root resorption or bone loss. Orthodontic
  treatment achieved stable occlusion, improved function, and satisfactory smile esthetics.
  Conclusions Orthodontics-assisted autogenous tooth transplantation may be a biologically
  favorable option for replacing a traumatized maxillary incisor in growing patients
  with crowding and malocclusion. Careful donor selection, coordinated orthodontic
  timing, and long-term follow-up are essential for stable functional and esthetic
  outcomes.

summaryDeep: 'Autogenous tooth transplantation (ATT) is an underused but biologically
  elegant option for replacing teeth in growing patients — it preserves alveolar bone,
  allows continued root development, and avoids implant placement before skeletal
  maturity. This case report describes a 10-year-old boy whose maxillary left central
  incisor had failed endodontic treatment and a periapical lesion; the mandibular
  right second premolar was transplanted into the incisor socket and the case was
  coordinated with full orthodontic treatment for bimaxillary crowding and Class II
  malocclusion. Pulp vitality could not be maintained post-transplant, so endodontic
  treatment was completed, but the tooth remained clinically and radiographically
  stable at long-term follow-up with healthy periodontium and no root resorption.
  As a single case report, it cannot establish efficacy, but it illustrates the planning
  principles — donor tooth stage (open apex preferred), socket preparation, splinting
  protocol, and orthodontic timing — that determine ATT success. For students: ATT
  is worth knowing as an option to discuss with paediatric trauma patients before
  committing to a space maintainer and future implant.'
