excerptFull: One persistent issue undermining the integrity of scientific communication
  is spin – the misleading presentation or interpretation of results to make findings
  appear more favourable, significant, or impactful than they truly are, thereby leading
  to bias. This article examines the nature of spin in scientific communication and
  highlights the pressing need for increased awareness and preventive measures. Spin
  represents a form of reporting bias that can mislead readers, clinicians, and policymakers.
  These seemingly small distortions add up and contribute to biased scientific records.
  In a context where research underpins medical treatments, spin is not merely an
  academic or inconsequential practice – it can have significant real-world implications.
  Addressing spin bias is essential for preserving the integrity, credibility, transparency,
  and utility of research. Spin can be addressed through institutional reforms, enhanced
  editorial and peer review oversight, and training researchers, reviewers, and editors
  in transparent and responsible reporting. While the complete elimination of spin
  may be unrealistic, systematic and sustained efforts to identify, mitigate, and
  discourage it are essential for preserving the integrity of scientific literature.
  Fostering a research culture grounded in integrity, rigor, and accountability will
  ensure that scientific outputs genuinely contribute to knowledge advancement and
  serve the broader benefit of society.

summaryDeep: 'Spin is not fraud, but it is systematic distortion: abstracts that emphasise
  p-values while burying effect sizes, conclusions that overreach the study design,
  and discussion sections that reframe null results as ''trends''. In dentistry, where
  much evidence comes from small RCTs and industry-funded trials, spin has outsized
  influence on clinical guidelines and product adoption. This commentary — paired
  with a companion piece in the same BDJ issue — makes the case that spin is not a
  marginal academic concern but a direct threat to patient care. The proposed remedies
  are familiar (CONSORT adherence, registered reports, post-publication review) but
  the framing is sharp: spin is a form of reporting bias with real-world consequences.
  For a dental student learning to critically appraise literature, this is required
  reading. The companion piece on ''Reducing the Spin'' (same topicThread) covers
  practical detection strategies.'
