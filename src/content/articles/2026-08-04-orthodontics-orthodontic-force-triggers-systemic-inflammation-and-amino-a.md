excerpt: Orthodontic treatment corrects various craniofacial malformations primarily
  through triggering active alveolar bone remodeling, however the potential impacts
  of this intervention on the systemic immune state have long been insufficiently
  explored. Here, we show that mechanical force applied during orthodontic treatment
  can trigger systemic inflammation dominated by adaptive immune responses. This response
  not only impairs bone repair at distant body sites but also causes temporary liver
  dysfunction resembling autoimmune conditions characterized by the expansion of CD69⁺
  B cells. Mechanistically, IgM produced by these activated B cells acts as a key
  pathogenic driver of systemic pathology. Inhibiting the activation of local B cells
  effectively reversed this immunopathology, underscoring the central role of B cell-derived
  IgM. We also verified that Piezo1⁺ macrophages function as vital mechanosensors,
  linking orthodontic mechanical stimulation to B cell activation and subsequent IgM
  release. Multi-organ metabolomic profiling revealed significant amino acid metabolic
  dysregulation, which further aggravated systemic inflammation. Crucially, supplementation
  with lysine and alanine alleviated orthodontic treatment-induced inflammation, restored
  liver function and promoted distal bone repair. Together, these findings uncover
  an unrecognized systemic immune-metabolic axis during orthodontic therapy and suggest

summaryDeep: 'Orthodontic treatment has always been understood as a local bone-remodelling
  event. This study challenges that framing: in a mouse model, mechanical force from
  tooth movement activated Piezo1-expressing macrophages at the site of force application,
  which in turn drove CD69⁺ B-cell expansion and systemic IgM release. The downstream
  consequences included impaired fracture repair at distant skeletal sites and transient
  liver dysfunction resembling autoimmune hepatitis. Multi-organ metabolomic profiling
  identified amino acid dysregulation — specifically depleted lysine and alanine —
  as an amplifier of the inflammatory cascade. Supplementing these amino acids normalised
  liver function, reduced systemic inflammation, and restored distal bone repair capacity.
  The study is entirely preclinical and the mouse-to-human translation is uncertain,
  but the mechanistic specificity is striking. For students, the key conceptual shift
  is that orthodontic force may not be immunologically neutral — a consideration that
  could eventually matter for patients with pre-existing autoimmune conditions or
  impaired healing.'
