excerpt: 'Preoperative information before orthognathic surgery is usually provided
  through verbal and written explanations; however, the effectiveness of supplementary
  visual tools, such as video animations and skull models, remains understudied. This
  study aimed to evaluate whether video animations and skull models effectively reduce
  preoperative anxiety in patients undergoing bimaxillary orthognathic surgery. A
  single-blinded randomized controlled trial was conducted at Ankara University Faculty
  of Dentistry between January 2024 and January 2025. Patients aged 18–36 years, classified
  as ASA I, and scheduled for elective bimaxillary orthognathic surgery were eligible.
  Exclusion criteria included previous orthognathic surgery, psychiatric disorders,
  or anxiolytic medication use. Patients were randomized equally into three groups:
  Group A, standard verbal information; Group B, verbal/video animation information;
  and Group C, verbal/3-D skull model information. The primary outcome was the change
  in State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-State score (STAI-I) between before information
  time (BIT) and one hour before surgery time (BST); secondary outcomes included changes

summaryDeep: 'Preoperative anxiety in orthognathic surgery is real and clinically
  relevant, and visual aids are widely assumed to help. This RCT from Ankara University
  — 30 patients, three arms, STAI-I as primary outcome — found no significant between-group
  difference in state anxiety reduction. Neither video animations nor 3D skull models
  outperformed standard verbal explanation. The sample size is small (10 per group),
  limiting power to detect modest effects, and the authors acknowledge this. However,
  the study is an RCT with a validated anxiety instrument, making it the highest-evidence
  item in today''s digest by design. The null result matters for resource allocation:
  investing in elaborate visual tools for surgical consent may not deliver the anxiety-reduction
  benefit assumed. For students learning patient communication, the takeaway is that
  the quality of verbal explanation may matter more than the medium. Future trials
  with larger samples and longer pre-surgical information windows are needed.'
