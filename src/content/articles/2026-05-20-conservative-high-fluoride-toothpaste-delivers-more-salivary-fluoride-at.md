summary: The timing and magnitude of salivary fluoride bioavailability after brushing
  determines how much remineralisation actually occurs overnight. This study compares
  5000 ppm and 1450 ppm dentifrices across diurnal and nocturnal periods, finding
  that high-fluoride paste produces significantly elevated nocturnal salivary fluoride
  — the window when salivary flow is lowest and fluoride retention highest. For high-caries-risk
  patients, the case for prescribing 5000 ppm just got stronger.
summaryDeep: 'Fluoride''s cariostatic mechanism depends on sustained low-level exposure
  rather than peak concentration, making the nocturnal period — characterised by minimal
  salivary flow and reduced clearance — the most pharmacologically important window.
  This crossover study measures salivary fluoride at multiple time points following
  evening brushing with 5000 ppm NaF and standard 1450 ppm NaF dentifrices in adult
  volunteers. High-fluoride paste produces approximately 2.5× higher nocturnal salivary
  fluoride concentrations, with the gap widening as the night progresses and salivary
  flow diminishes. Daytime differences are smaller and less clinically meaningful.
  The study also confirms that rinsing behaviour after brushing is the dominant modifying
  variable — spitting without rinsing amplifies the nocturnal fluoride advantage of
  high-concentration paste. For students: this is the mechanistic justification for
  prescribing Duraphat 5000 to high-risk patients and instructing them explicitly
  not to rinse after evening brushing.'
