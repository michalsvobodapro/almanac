excerpt: Patients with a midline diastema smaller than 2 mm are often treated with
  orthodontic elastics applied on composite resin esthetic buttons bonded to incisors
  or incorporated in clear removable trays. However, these methods of applying elastics
  for diastema closure have shortcomings, including the additional clinical steps
  of etching the facial tooth surface and bonding for composite resin buttons; occlusal
  derangement and dependence on patient compliance for trays; and compromised esthetics
  for both treatment approaches. This article presents an alternative technique that
  overcomes the limitations of those methods by applying orthodontic elastics directly
  on the central incisors without the use of esthetic buttons or removable trays.
  The technique can lead to tipping of the incisors and therefore is restricted to
  treatment of diastemas up to 2 mm. The technique is straightforward to perform with
  readily available materials and can be performed by a general dentist. Once the
  desired space closure is achieved, the corrected position of the incisors can be
  retained with a resin-bonded splint to counter labial tipping movement and aid in
  root alignment. In some cases, esthetic restorations such as laminate veneers are
  indicated to close the redistributed interdental spaces, and the contact areas of
  the restorations could serve as a form of retention.

summaryDeep: 'Midline diastema closure is a common request in general practice, and
  the existing toolkit — composite resin buttons with elastics, or clear aligner trays
  — each carries real drawbacks: buttons require acid etching and bonding steps; trays
  depend entirely on patient compliance and can disrupt occlusion. This technique
  report proposes a third path: elastics applied directly to the contact areas of
  the central incisors, exploiting natural tooth morphology as the attachment point.
  The method is restricted to diastemas ≤2 mm because larger spaces risk unacceptable
  labial tipping of the incisors, and the authors are explicit about this limitation.
  Once closure is achieved, a resin-bonded splint is placed to counter the tipping
  vector and stabilize root alignment; in cases with redistributed interdental spaces,
  laminate veneers can serve dual esthetic and retention functions. This is a technique
  report, not a clinical trial — there are no outcome data, no failure rates, no follow-up
  periods. Its value is practical: a lower-barrier option for GDPs managing straightforward
  diastemas without orthodontic referral.'
