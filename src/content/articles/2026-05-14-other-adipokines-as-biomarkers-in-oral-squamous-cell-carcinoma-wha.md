excerptFull: To identify and summarize the types of adipokines associated with Oral
  Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC) pathogenesis and prognosis. A comprehensive literature
  search was done involving Scopus, PubMed, and Embase. Studies involving adipokines
  in OSCC conducted in humans were included. Adipokines have diverse and stage-dependent
  effects on OSCC, including regulation of inflammation, angiogenesis, hypoxia, and
  tumour progression. Adipokines such as chemerin, resistin, leptin, and apelin contribute
  to tumour progression. Zinc alpha-2-glycoprotein and adiponectin were found to be
  antitumourigenic in OSCC. Methodological heterogeneity and a lack of standardized
  outcome measures limit comparability across studies to establish the definitive
  role of adipokines in OSCC. Adipokines shape OSCC biology through diverse, stage-specific
  mechanisms, making them potential biomarkers of disease progression. The adipokines
  reported in OSCC require validation through long-term prospective studies and addressing
  methodological heterogeneity to serve as prognostic markers.

summary: A systematic literature sweep across PubMed, Scopus, and Embase maps which
  adipokines drive OSCC progression and which suppress it. Leptin, resistin, chemerin,
  and apelin correlate with tumour advancement; adiponectin and zinc alpha-2-glycoprotein
  appear antitumourigenic. Methodological heterogeneity prevents firm clinical conclusions
  — but the biomarker framework is now clearly drawn.
summaryDeep: 'This narrative review consolidates human-study evidence on adipokines
  — signalling proteins secreted by adipose tissue — and their roles in oral squamous
  cell carcinoma biology. The pro-tumourigenic cluster (leptin, resistin, chemerin,
  apelin) promotes inflammation, angiogenesis, and hypoxia signalling in a stage-dependent
  manner, meaning the same molecule can behave differently early versus late in disease.
  On the other side, adiponectin and zinc alpha-2-glycoprotein consistently show antitumourigenic
  effects, suggesting potential protective or therapeutic angles. The authors are
  candid about the ceiling: no standardised outcome measures, small heterogeneous
  cohorts, and a near-total absence of prospective longitudinal data mean none of
  these can yet serve as validated prognostic markers. For a dental student, the clinical
  takeaway is conceptual — understanding that systemic metabolic status (obesity,
  metabolic syndrome) feeds directly into OSCC biology via adipokine signalling, reinforcing
  why a thorough medical history matters at every oral cancer screening.'
