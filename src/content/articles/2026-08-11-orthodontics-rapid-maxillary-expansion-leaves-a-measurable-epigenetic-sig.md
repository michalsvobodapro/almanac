excerpt: Rapid maxillary expansion (RME) is a widely used orthodontic intervention
  for correcting transverse maxillary deficiencies. By mechanically separating the
  midpalatal suture, RME improves occlusal relationships and induces tissue repair
  processes similar to fracture healing. Inflammation and bone regeneration following
  fracture are regulated by epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation. However,
  the effects of RME-induced mechanical stimulation on epigenetic dynamics in humans
  remain unclear. We evaluated genome-wide DNA methylation changes in saliva associated
  with RME treatment. Twenty patients undergoing orthodontic treatment with RME and
  four untreated controls were enrolled. Saliva samples were collected before (T0)
  and after treatment (T1), and genome-wide DNA methylation profiling was performed
  using the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip. Differential methylation
  analysis was conducted using linear models accounting for within-subject correlations.

  (false discovery rate < 0.05), whereas no significant changes were observed in controls.
  The differentially methylated CpG sites were annotated to genes associated with

summaryDeep: 'Rapid maxillary expansion is a routine orthodontic procedure, but its
  molecular biology beyond gross bone remodelling has been poorly characterised. This
  study enrolled 20 RME patients and 4 untreated controls, collecting saliva before
  and after treatment for genome-wide DNA methylation profiling using the Illumina
  EPIC v2.0 array. RME was associated with 164 differentially methylated CpG sites
  (FDR < 0.05); controls showed none. Annotated genes cluster around inflammatory
  signalling and metabolic processes — consistent with the tissue injury and repair
  cascade triggered by midpalatal suture separation. The clinical implications are
  currently indirect: salivary epigenetic profiling is not a chairside tool, and the
  control group (n=4) is too small to draw firm conclusions about specificity. However,
  the study opens a genuinely new line of inquiry — could epigenetic markers in saliva
  predict treatment response or identify patients at risk of relapse? The finding
  also raises a broader question about the long-term biological consequences of mechanical
  orthodontic forces that deserves follow-up in larger cohorts.'
