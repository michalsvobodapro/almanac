  navigation systems (infrared-active, infrared-passive, visible light-passive) against
  freehand access under microscope guidance. All DNS types reduced angular deviation

summaryDeep: 'Ninety-six extracted mandibular molars were embedded in plaster, CBCT-scanned,
  and randomly assigned to four groups: three dynamic navigation systems and a freehand
  control using microscope plus CBCT reference. DNS groups used fiducial markers for
  real-time bur tracking. Angular deviation in DNS groups ranged 0.59°–0.64° versus
  11.03° freehand — a reduction of roughly 94–95%. Three-dimensional entry and target
  deviations were also significantly lower in all DNS groups. Success rate reached
  99.54% for DNS versus 93.06% freehand (p=0.004). Crucially, the three DNS technologies
  performed identically, suggesting the navigation principle matters more than the
  specific light source. Tooth structure loss and instrumentation time were not significantly
  different. The in vitro setting limits direct clinical translation, but the magnitude
  of accuracy gains is hard to dismiss, particularly for calcified or aberrant canals
  where freehand access carries the highest risk of perforation.'
