summary: Smoking is the dominant modifiable risk factor for periodontitis, partly
  through oxidative stress pathways that vitamin C could theoretically counteract.
  This rat study confirms that vitamin C supplementation reduces gingival oxidative
  stress markers and inflammatory cytokines in smoke-exposed animals with experimental
  periodontitis — but the effect on bone loss is limited. Antioxidant supplementation
  is not a substitute for smoking cessation.
summaryDeep: 'The biological rationale for vitamin C in periodontal disease management
  is sound: ascorbate is a key antioxidant in gingival tissue, smokers are systemically
  depleted of it, and oxidative stress drives the cytokine cascade that mediates alveolar
  bone loss. This animal study induces periodontitis via ligature placement in cigarette-smoke-exposed
  rats, then randomises to vitamin C supplementation or control, measuring gingival
  IL-1β, TNF-α, MDA (lipid peroxidation marker), and alveolar bone loss at 30 days.
  Vitamin C significantly reduces oxidative stress markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines.
  However, alveolar bone loss is only modestly attenuated and does not reach statistical
  significance in all comparisons. The translational ceiling here is real: oxidative
  stress is one pathway among many in smoking-associated periodontitis, and systemic
  antioxidant supplementation cannot compensate for the vasoconstriction, impaired
  neutrophil function, and altered microbiome that smoking also produces. Clinically,
  vitamin C optimisation in smoking patients is reasonable adjunct advice — but the
  conversation must centre on cessation.'
