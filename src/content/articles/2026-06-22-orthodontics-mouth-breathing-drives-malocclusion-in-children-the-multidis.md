excerpt: 'Resume. This scientific article is devoted to the study of the role and
  mechanisms of the influence of incorrect breathing habits on the development of
  orthodontic pathologies in children. Breathing disorders, particularly mouth breathing,
  are one of the current problems in modern orthodontics, with a prevalence among
  the pediatric population ranging from 11% to 56.8%. Analysis of literature data
  shows that mouth breathing leads to narrowing of the dental arches, increased palatal
  depth, development of crossbite, and Class II skeletal occlusion. Incorrect breathing
  habits disrupt muscle balance, cause a low tongue position, and alter the direction
  of facial skeletal growth. The article examines the relationship between breathing
  disorders caused by adenotonsillar hypertrophy, allergic rhinitis, and other obstructive
  factors with the development of orthodontic pathologies. It is substantiated that
  early diagnosis and a comprehensive approach involving an orthodontist, otorhinolaryngologist,
  speech therapist, and myofunctional therapist are of great importance for preventing
  pathological changes and improving treatment effectiveness. Keywords: incorrect
  breathing, mouth breathing, orthodontic pathologies, malocclusion, adenotonsillar
  hypertrophy, myofunctional therapy, craniofacial growth, pediatric dentistry.'

summary: A literature review synthesizes evidence that mouth breathing — prevalent
  in 11–56.8% of children — causes narrowed dental arches, increased palatal depth,
  crossbite, and Class II skeletal patterns via disrupted muscle balance and altered
  craniofacial growth vectors. Early multidisciplinary management (orthodontist, ENT,

  data, but a useful clinical framework.
summaryDeep: 'Mouth breathing is one of the most common yet underaddressed etiologic
  factors in pediatric malocclusion. This review consolidates the mechanistic pathway:
  adenotonsillar hypertrophy or allergic rhinitis forces oral breathing, which drops
  tongue posture, removes transverse expansion forces on the palate, and redirects
  vertical facial growth — producing the classic ''adenoid face'' with narrow arches,
  anterior open bite tendency, and Class II skeletal relationship. The prevalence
  range (11–56.8%) reflects diagnostic heterogeneity across studies. The article''s
  strongest contribution is the interdisciplinary framework: orthodontic treatment
  alone is insufficient if the underlying airway obstruction persists, and ENT referral
  for adenoid/tonsil assessment should precede or accompany arch expansion. Myofunctional
  therapy targets the muscle imbalance directly. Evidence grade is narrative review
  with no meta-analytic synthesis, so effect sizes are not quantified. For a Prague
  student, this is a reminder that the orthodontic examination should include a breathing
  assessment and that early referral pathways matter.'
