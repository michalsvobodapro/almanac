excerpt: 'Abstract Objectives This retrospective study aimed to identify risk indicators
  for erosive tooth wear (ETW) in patients from the Midwest US. Materials and methods
  Clinical records from 2019 to 2023 were reviewed from 2,482 adult patients diagnosed
  with ETW at the University of Iowa College of Dentistry. A control group of 2,967
  patients without ETW was randomly selected from the same timeframe. Data on demographic
  information, self-reported medical history, and oral health condition/habits were
  extracted. Logistic regression analysis (α = 0.05) was used to evaluate associations
  between ETW and potential risk indicators. Results ETW was significantly associated
  with age (35–54 years: OR = 1.24; p = 0.03), male sex (OR = 1.43; p &lt; 0.001),
  parafunctional habits (OR = 1.44; p &lt; 0.001), and eating disorders (OR = 1.90;
  p = 0.014). No significant associations were found for Social Deprivation Index
  (SDI), sleep apnea, dry mouth, toothbrushing frequency, alcohol use, or GERD. Conclusions
  Within the limitations of this retrospective study, age (35–54 years), male sex,

  and/or anorexia nervosa) were identified as significant risk indicators for erosive

  four significant risk indicators for erosive tooth wear: age 35–54 (OR 1.24), male
  sex (OR 1.43), parafunctional habits (OR 1.44), and eating disorders (OR 1.90).

summaryDeep: 'Reviewing records from 2,482 ETW patients and 2,967 controls seen between
  2019 and 2023, this study applies logistic regression to a dataset large enough
  to meaningfully test associations that smaller studies have only speculated about.
  The eating-disorder signal is the sharpest finding — an OR of 1.90 for bulimia/anorexia
  is clinically actionable and should prompt systematic screening questions in that
  demographic. The null results are equally important: GERD, long assumed to be a
  major driver, did not reach significance here, which challenges the reflex of attributing
  palatal erosion to reflux without further workup. Parafunctional habits (bruxism,
  clenching, grinding) emerged as a strong independent factor, reinforcing the need
  to distinguish attrition from erosion in the differential. The retrospective design
  and self-reported medical history introduce bias, but the sample size lends the
  ORs reasonable stability. For a student in clinic, this is a clean evidence base
  for structuring your ETW risk interview.'
