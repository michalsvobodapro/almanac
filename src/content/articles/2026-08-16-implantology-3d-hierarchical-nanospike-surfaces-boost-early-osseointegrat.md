excerpt: Anisotropic titanium nano-surfaces produced by alkali etching exert diverse
  biological effects through physicochemical cues derived from anisotropically distributed
  nanospikes. These effects can be further enhanced by hierarchically superimposing
  nanostructures onto micro-roughened surfaces. This study aimed to investigate the
  effects of micro/nano three-dimensionally hierarchical titanium surfaces on the
  osteoblastic functions and osseointegration of dental implants. Three-dimensionally
  nano-roughened titanium surfaces fabricated using a combination of hot acid and
  alkaline treatments showed a higher density and anisotropic distribution of nanospikes
  than that on conventional nano-roughened surfaces, while exhibiting key physicochemical
  features similar to those of conventional nano-roughened surfaces, including superhydrophilicity
  and negative surface potential associated with surface hydroxyl groups. These three-dimensionally
  nano-roughened surfaces significantly enhanced bovine serum albumin adsorption.
  They also promoted the proliferation and osteoblastic differentiation of mouse bone
  marrow stromal cells compared with conventional micro-roughened or nano-roughened
  titanium surfaces. In vivo, titanium implants with three-dimensional nano-surfaces
  showed significantly greater osseointegration strength than that of micro-roughened
  implants when placed in the rat maxillary molar region. This enhancement was associated

summaryDeep: 'Surface engineering of dental implants is an active frontier, and this
  study adds a new layer: three-dimensional hierarchical surfaces that superimpose
  anisotropic nanospikes onto micro-roughened titanium. The fabrication uses a combination
  of hot acid and alkaline treatments, producing higher nanospike density than conventional
  nano-roughening while preserving superhydrophilicity and negative surface potential.
  In vitro, bovine serum albumin adsorption was enhanced, and mouse bone marrow stromal
  cells showed greater proliferation and osteoblastic differentiation. In vivo, pull-out
  strength in rat maxillary molar sites was significantly higher than micro-roughened
  controls, associated with earlier bone formation and greater bone-implant contact.
  The evidence grade is low — rat models and cell culture — and the translation to
  human clinical outcomes requires human trials. Still, the mechanistic logic is sound,
  and this surface type may eventually reach commercial development. Worth knowing
  as a concept, not yet as a clinical recommendation.'
