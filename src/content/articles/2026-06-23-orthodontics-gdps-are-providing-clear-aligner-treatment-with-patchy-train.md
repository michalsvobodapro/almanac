excerptFull: Aims This study explored the training and working experiences of general
  dental practitioners (GDPs) in England in providing clear aligner treatment (CAT)
  to adult patients. Methods A mixed methods approach was adopted, including an online
  questionnaire and semi-structured interviews of GDPs in England providing CAT. Quantitative
  data were analysed descriptively using MS Excel and qualitative data using framework
  methodology. Results One hundred completed surveys were received, and 16 interviews
  were conducted. Most GDPs reported having attained aligner certification (n = 86)
  and nearly one-third (n = 31) gained some theoretical knowledge of CAT but highlighted
  key shortcomings of the training courses. GDPs felt confident in treating mild or
  moderate malocclusion traits and expressed several reasons for choosing a specific
  CAT and digital scanner and provider. Those GDPs qualified more than ten years could
  apply complex treatment mechanics. Laboratory-made fixed retainers were of higher
  preference following CAT, especially in complex malocclusions with high risk of
  relapse. Conclusions GDPs' limited understanding and diverse practices in CAT provision,
  suggest the need for policymakers to review the undergraduate dental curriculum
  and for educators to ensure the inclusion of theoretical and practical aspects of
  CAT, together with the ongoing support of specialist in orthodontics.

summaryDeep: Clear aligner treatment has migrated rapidly from specialist to general
  practice, but the training infrastructure has not kept pace. This UK mixed-methods
  study — 100 surveys and 16 semi-structured interviews — reveals that 86% of GDPs
  hold some form of aligner certification, yet most describe those courses as theoretically
  thin. Confidence was appropriate for mild-to-moderate malocclusion but eroded sharply
  for complex cases; GDPs with over ten years of experience were better equipped to
  handle advanced mechanics. Scanner and provider choice was often driven by commercial
  relationships rather than clinical evidence. Retention protocol varied widely, though
  laboratory-made fixed retainers were preferred post-treatment in complex cases.
  The study is UK-specific, but the pattern — commercial certification filling a curriculum
  vacuum — is recognisable across Europe, including Czech dental education. The authors
  call for undergraduate curriculum reform and structured specialist support, which
  maps onto ongoing EFP and national society discussions about scope of practice.
