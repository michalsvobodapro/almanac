excerpt: 'AbstractIntroduction: Instrument separation during root canal treatment
  is a recognized procedural complication that may compromise canal debridement and
  treatment outcome. Management depends on the location of the fragment, with coronal
  third fragments showing higher success rates for conservative approaches such as
  bypass and ultrasonic retrieval.Case Presentation: Two clinical cases with separated
  instruments in the coronal third of molar canals are presented. Both patients had
  a history of previously initiated root canal treatment.Radiographic examination
  confirmed the presence of fractured instruments obstructing canal access. After
  achieving isolation and access refinement, bypass was successfully performed using
  #8 and #10 K-files to establish canal patency and glide path. This was followed
  by ultrasonic activation using the Endo-X ultrasonic system under continuous sodium
  hypochlorite irrigation, which facilitated safe retrieval of the fragments. Subsequent
  biomechanical preparation was completed using rotary instrumentation, followed by
  obturation and post-endodontic restoration.Conclusion: The combination of bypass
  technique and ultrasonic retrieval provides a conservative, effective, and predictable
  approach for managing separated instruments in the coronal third. This method preserves
  radicular dentin and enables successful completion of endodontic therapy.'

summaryDeep: 'Instrument separation is one of the more anxiety-inducing complications
  in endodontics, and management decisions hinge heavily on fragment location. Coronal-third
  separations carry the best prognosis for retrieval because access is direct and
  dentin sacrifice is limited. In both cases presented, #8 and #10 K-files established
  patency and a glide path alongside the fragment before ultrasonic trephination freed
  it. The Endo-X ultrasonic system under continuous sodium hypochlorite irrigation
  provided both mechanical energy and chemical lubrication. The conservative approach
  — preserving radicular dentin rather than aggressive trephination — is the correct
  hierarchy, and these cases illustrate it cleanly. The evidence level is low (n=2
  case series), so this is technique illustration rather than comparative evidence.
  Paired with the retreatment technique study in today''s digest, it rounds out a
  practical endodontic retreatment picture: rotary systems for gutta-percha, ultrasonics
  for separated instruments.'
