excerpt: 'Objective To evaluate the efficacy of air-polishing on color change and
  surface roughness of resin composite and enamel specimens after standardized cigarette
  smoke exposure. Methods Sixty composite/enamel specimens were exposed daily to cigarette
  smoke in an automated chamber over four 14-day cycles (five cigarettes/day). After
  each cycle, specimens were cleaned using air-polishing with erythritol or sodium
  bicarbonate powder, or a rubber cup and pumice (control). Color changes (ΔE) were
  assessed spectrophotometrically, and surface roughness was measured profilometrically
  before and after each cycle. Results All cleaning methods significantly reduced
  tobacco staining on composite and enamel; however, none restored the original color.
  After four cycles, median [IQR] ΔE values for resin composites did not differ significantly
  among groups (erythritol: 26.17 [21.62-27.52]; sodium bicarbonate: 10.28 [7.44-27.77];
  control: 24.56 [19.22-26.77]; p > 0.05). Surface roughness increased significantly
  in all groups, with the greatest increase following sodium bicarbonate air-polishing
  (p Conclusions Erythritol air-polishing effectively reduced discoloration with minimal
  surface alteration, whereas sodium bicarbonate caused significant roughening. Clinical
  significance Cigarette smoke causes clinically perceptible composite discoloration
  that cannot be completely eliminated. Erythritol represents a safer polishing option
  than sodium bicarbonate.'

summaryDeep: 'Tobacco staining on resin composite is a daily clinical problem, and
  the choice of polishing abrasive matters because rougher surfaces re-stain faster
  and harbour more biofilm. This study exposed 60 composite/enamel specimens to five
  cigarettes per day in an automated chamber over four 14-day cycles, then cleaned
  with erythritol air-polishing, sodium bicarbonate air-polishing, or rubber cup with
  pumice. All three methods significantly reduced ΔE values, but none fully restored
  baseline colour after four cycles — a clinically important finding for patient counselling.
  Crucially, sodium bicarbonate caused the greatest increase in surface roughness
  (statistically significant vs. erythritol and control), while erythritol produced
  minimal surface alteration. The in-vitro setting cannot replicate intraoral saliva
  buffering or polishing angulation variability, and the composite tested may not
  generalise to all formulations. Still, the directional signal is clear and consistent
  with prior erythritol literature: switch to erythritol when polishing composite
  in smokers.'
