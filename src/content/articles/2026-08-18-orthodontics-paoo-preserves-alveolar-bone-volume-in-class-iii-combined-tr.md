excerpt: Introduction Patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion often have thin
  anterior alveolar bone and an increased periodontal risk. This study evaluated 3-dimensional
  (3D) alveolar bone volume changes in patients treated with or without periodontally
  accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO) using a deep learning-assisted cone-beam
  computed tomography (CBCT) approach. Methods A total of 42 adults undergoing orthodontic-orthognathic
  treatment were divided into a PAOO group (group S, n = 21) and a matched non-PAOO
  control group (group NS, n = 21). CBCT scans were obtained at baseline (T1) and
  posttreatment (T3), with a 6-month post-PAOO scan (T2) for group S. Labial, lingual,
  and total alveolar bone volumes were quantified using a deep learning-assisted automated
  segmentation tool. Linear mixed-effects models were used to analyze longitudinal
  changes and treatment effects, adjusting for baseline root length. Results In group
  S, labial alveolar bone volume increased from T1 (42.94 ± 23.15 mm 3 ) to T3 (84.15
  ± 36.82 mm 3 ; P 3 at T3 vs 114.20 ± 66.86 mm 3 at T1; P 3 ; P Conclusions PAOO
  was associated with increased and maintained labial alveolar bone volume, which
  may help preserve total alveolar support during combined treatment. Deep learning-assisted
  CBCT segmentation can serve as an efficient volumetric evaluation tool.

summaryDeep: 'Skeletal Class III patients are notoriously at risk for thin labial
  alveolar bone during combined orthodontic-orthognathic treatment. This prospective
  matched cohort (n=42) compared PAOO versus standard treatment, using a deep learning-automated
  CBCT segmentation tool to quantify labial, lingual, and total alveolar bone volumes
  at baseline, 6 months post-PAOO, and post-treatment. The PAOO group saw labial bone
  volume nearly double (42.9 → 84.2 mm³), while the control group experienced significant
  bone loss. Total alveolar support was maintained in the PAOO group but declined
  in controls. The deep learning segmentation pipeline is the methodological highlight:
  it enables reproducible 3D volumetric tracking that linear CBCT measurements cannot
  capture. Limitations include the moderate sample size and single-centre design.
  Still, for clinicians managing Class III patients with thin anterior bone, this
  adds meaningful prospective evidence that PAOO is not just accelerating tooth movement
  — it is actively building a better bone envelope.'
