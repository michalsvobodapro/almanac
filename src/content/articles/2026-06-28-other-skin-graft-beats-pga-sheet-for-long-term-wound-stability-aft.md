excerpt: Skin grafting and polyglycolic acid (PGA) sheet application are two established
  mucosal repair techniques for early-stage oral cancer defects, yet quantitative
  long-term comparative data are lacking. To compare long-term wound contracture and
  postoperative complications of two methods for cT1-2 oral cancer mucosal defects.
  In this retrospective cohort study, 53 patients who underwent mucosal repair with
  skin graft (n = 25) or PGA sheet (n = 28) were followed for long-term wound shrinkage
  (median, 40.0 months), graft failure/necrosis, postoperative bleeding, and tongue
  tethering. Wound area decreased to 25.0% (interquartile range [IQR], 15.5-36.3)
  of the initial defect in the skin graft group versus 12.6% (IQR, 9.2-17.9) in the
  PGA sheet group (p = 0.0019). Multivariable analysis identified PGA sheet application
  (p = 0.0015) and adjuvant radiation (p = 0.0056) as independent predictors of greater
  wound shrinkage. Postoperative bleeding (21.4% vs. 8.0%) and tongue tethering (32.1%
  vs. 8.0%, p = 0.043) were more frequent in the PGA sheet group. Skin grafting offers
  superior long-term dimensional stability and fewer functional complications for
  cT1-2 oral cancer defects. PGA sheets may be preferred when operative time or donor-site
  morbidity is a priority, providing practical guidance for reconstructive planning.

summaryDeep: 'Mucosal repair after early oral cancer resection influences both oncologic
  surveillance and functional outcomes, yet comparative long-term data between skin
  grafting and polyglycolic acid (PGA) sheet application have been sparse. This retrospective
  cohort followed 53 patients (25 skin graft, 28 PGA) for a median of 40 months, measuring
  wound area as a percentage of initial defect size. PGA sheets contracted significantly
  more (to 12.6% of original area vs 25% for skin graft, p = 0.0019), and multivariable
  analysis confirmed PGA application and adjuvant radiation as independent predictors
  of greater shrinkage. Tongue tethering — a functionally significant complication
  — was four times more frequent in the PGA group (32.1% vs 8.0%, p = 0.043). Postoperative
  bleeding was also numerically higher with PGA. The retrospective design and modest
  sample size limit certainty, but the functional complication signal is clinically
  meaningful. For students on oral surgery or head-and-neck rotations: skin grafting
  should be the default for floor-of-mouth and tongue defects where tethering risk
  is high; PGA is a reasonable alternative when operative time or donor-site morbidity
  is the overriding concern.'
