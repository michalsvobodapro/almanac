summary: A causal inference analysis of 2,941 Australian children finds that twice-daily
  toothbrushing at age 2–3 is associated with a 27% reduction in caries incidence
  through age 14–15 (aOR 0.73, 95% CI 0.63–0.85). Five analytical methods — including
  propensity score matching, IPTW, and TMLE — all converge on the same effect size,
  making confounding an unlikely explanation. The protective effect is strongest in
  non-disadvantaged areas, flagging an equity gap that prevention programmes need
  to address.
summaryDeep: Using the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (LSAC B-cohort, n=2,941),
  researchers emulated a target trial to estimate the causal effect of twice-daily
  toothbrushing at age 2–3 on incident caries through adolescence. Caries incidence
  was 48.6% in the twice-daily group versus 56.8% in the less-frequent group — a 27%
  reduction in adjusted odds that held across multivariable logistic regression, propensity
  score matching, IPTW, TMLE, and g-computation (OR range 0.72–0.75). A dose-response
  gradient was statistically significant (p-trend <0.001). Critically, the protective
  effect was attenuated in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, suggesting that
  brushing frequency alone cannot overcome structural barriers to oral health. For
  clinical practice, this is the strongest longitudinal evidence to date that early-life
  oral hygiene counselling — specifically the twice-daily habit — has measurable,
  durable benefits. The equity finding is a direct prompt for targeted community programmes
  rather than generic advice.
