excerpt: 'This cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the influence of vitamin D
  and glycaemic control on the severity of periodontitis in Cameroonian type 2 diabetics
  (T2D) and to identify the determinants of severe periodontitis (SP). Vitamin D concentration
  and blood glucose control were determined by measuring serum levels of 25(OH)D and
  HbA1c. Periodontal examination was conducted. Fisher''s exact test was used to compare
  proportions and Mann Whitney''s test to compare medians. Univariate and multivariate
  analyses were performed to determine influencing factors on the prevalence of SP.
  82 T2D patients were recruited. The median age of participants was 60 (15) years,
  and 54.9% of them had a glycated hemoglobin level ≥ 7%. T2D patients with SP had
  significantly lower median serum 25(OH)D levels than those without SP (20.5 (7.7)
  ng/mL versus 25.2 (8.8) ng/mL, p = 0.028). After logistic regression, only poor
  glycaemic control and age were significantly associated with the severity of periodontitis
  (OR: 6.81 [2.07-22.35] p = 0.002; OR: 1.07 [1.01-1.12] p = 0.013), respectively.
  Our study highlights the negative impact of poor glycaemic control and advanced
  age on the periodontium. The role of vitamin D on the severity of periodontitis
  still needs to be defined more precisely in future studies.'

summaryDeep: 'The bidirectional relationship between diabetes and periodontitis is
  well-established, but the relative contributions of glycaemic control versus micronutrient
  status — particularly vitamin D — remain contested. This study enrolled 82 type
  2 diabetic patients, measured serum 25(OH)D and HbA1c, and performed full periodontal
  examination. On univariate analysis, patients with severe periodontitis had significantly
  lower vitamin D levels (20.5 vs. 25.2 ng/mL, p=0.028). However, after logistic regression,
  only poor glycaemic control (OR 6.81) and age (OR 1.07) remained significant — vitamin
  D dropped out. The sample size of 82 is underpowered to detect a modest vitamin
  D effect after adjustment for confounders, so the null finding on vitamin D should
  not be over-interpreted. For clinical practice, the message is unchanged: optimizing
  HbA1c before and during periodontal treatment is the lever with the strongest evidence.
  Vitamin D supplementation as a periodontal adjunct remains investigational.'
