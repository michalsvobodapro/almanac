excerpt: 'OBJECTIVE: To comparatively evaluate the effects of four low-abrasive air-polishing
  powders (glycine, erythritol, trehalose, and sodium bicarbonate) on root surface
  roughness and substance loss in an ex vivo model. MATERIALS AND METHODS: 210 specimens
  from 105 human teeth were randomly allocated to four treatment groups (n = 50);
  10 additional specimens served for measurement error analysis. Air-polishing was
  performed using glycine (25 μm), erythritol (14 μm), trehalose (30 μm), or sodium
  bicarbonate (40 μm). Three-dimensional surface topography was assessed by laser
  scanning microscopy before and after treatment using ISO 25,178 roughness parameters.
  Data were analyzed using rank-based ANCOVA with tooth type and surface location
  as covariates. RESULTS: p = 0.217). Glycine (8.23 [5.45; 14.13] µm) and erythritol
  (6.65 [4.61; 10.22] µm) produced significantly higher values than trehalose (4.38
  [2.87; 5.42] µm) and sodium bicarbonate (3.82 [2.83; 5.79] µm). Erythritol generated
  the most pronounced increases in valley depth and peak height. All groups showed
  negative skewness trends. No significant differences in substance loss were detected.
  CONCLUSION: Glycine and erythritol produced greater surface roughness than trehalose
  and sodium bicarbonate under the tested device configuration, while all powders
  demonstrated minimal substance loss, supporting their safety for subgingival application.

summaryDeep: Air-polishing with low-abrasive powders is increasingly used for subgingival
  biofilm removal, and glycine and erythritol have dominated the literature as the
  'gentle' options. This ex vivo study of 210 specimens from 105 human teeth challenges
  that assumption at the surface-topography level. Using laser scanning microscopy
  and ISO 25178 3D roughness parameters, glycine (median Sq 8.23 µm) and erythritol
  (6.65 µm) produced significantly rougher surfaces than trehalose (4.38 µm) and sodium
  bicarbonate (3.82 µm). Crucially, substance loss was minimal and statistically equivalent
  across all groups — so the safety argument for subgingival use holds. The finding
  that erythritol, despite its smaller particle size (14 µm vs. glycine's 25 µm),
  produced greater valley depth and peak height than sodium bicarbonate (40 µm) underscores
  that particle size is not the sole determinant of surface effect. For clinical practice,
  this suggests that powder selection for root surface air-polishing should consider
  surface topography outcomes, not just particle size labeling. Rougher root surfaces
  could theoretically accelerate biofilm reattachment, though in vivo confirmation
  is needed.
