excerpt: Transverse maxillary deficiency associated with posterior crossbite is one
  of the most common dentofacial deformities. Orthodontic treatment modalities such
  as rapid palatal expansion and rapid maxillary expansion are widely used to correct
  this condition. After the completion of skeletal maturation, non-surgical palatal
  expansion results predominantly in dental movement within the alveolar process rather
  than true skeletal expansion. This may lead to numerous complications, including
  molar extrusion and periodontal problems. Therefore, combined surgical and orthodontic
  treatment is considered a more effective and safer approach for correcting transverse
  discrepancies in adult patients. Segmental Le Fort I osteotomy and surgically assisted
  rapid maxillary expansion are the most commonly employed surgical techniques for
  the treatment of transverse maxillary discrepancies.

summaryDeep: 'Once the mid-palatal suture fuses — typically in the late teens — conventional
  rapid palatal expansion appliances cannot generate true skeletal widening. The forces
  instead tip the buccal teeth, risking buccal bone dehiscence, gingival recession,
  and molar extrusion. SARME addresses this by surgically releasing the sutural and
  cortical resistance before applying the expansion device, achieving genuine skeletal
  movement with lower periodontal risk. This review covers patient selection, the
  surgical technique (pterygomaxillary and lateral nasal wall osteotomies), activation
  protocols, retention, and complications including relapse, nerve paresthesia, and
  asymmetric expansion. The evidence base for SARME is largely cohort-level with no
  large RCTs, so the review is more of a structured clinical guide than a meta-analytic
  summary. For orthodontic students, the key takeaway is the age threshold: if a patient
  presents with posterior crossbite and is past skeletal maturity, the orthodontic-only
  route is a compromise, not a treatment.'
