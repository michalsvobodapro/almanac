excerpt: Background Early childhood caries (ECC) is one of the most prevalent chronic
  diseases in children and is associated with inflammatory and oxidative processes.
  This study aimed to evaluate salivary oxidative stress biomarkers and oxidative
  DNA damage in children with ECC and severe ECC (S-ECC). Methods This cross-sectional
  study included 90 children aged 2-6 years (control, n = 29; ECC, n = 30; S-ECC,
  n = 31).Salivary total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidant status (TOS), oxidative
  stress index (OSI), arylesterase (ARES), asprosin, and 8-hydroxy-2'-deoxyguanosine
  (8-OHdG) levels were analyzed. Correlation and multivariate regression analyses
  were performed to evaluate the associations between biomarkers and caries severity.
  Results TAS levels differed significantly among the groups (p = 0.014) and were
  significantly lower in the ECC group than in the control and S-ECC groups. In contrast,
  8-OHdG levels were significantly higher in the S-ECC group than in the control group
  (p = 0.013). No significant differences were observed among the groups regarding
  asprosin (p = 0.901), TOS (p = 0.865), OSI (p = 0.422), or ARES levels (p = 0.471).

summary: In 90 children aged 2–6 years, severe ECC was independently associated with
  higher salivary 8-OHdG (oxidative DNA damage marker), while ECC without severity
  was linked to lower total antioxidant status. The findings suggest ECC is not merely
  a localized infection but involves systemic oxidative stress, with severity-dependent
  biomarker profiles.
summaryDeep: 'Early childhood caries research has focused heavily on microbial and
  dietary factors; this cross-sectional study adds an oxidative stress dimension.
  Ninety children (control, ECC, S-ECC) were profiled for six salivary biomarkers
  including TAS, TOS, OSI, arylesterase, asprosin, and 8-OHdG. The key finding: S-ECC
  specifically elevates 8-OHdG (oxidative DNA damage), while ECC lowers TAS (antioxidant
  capacity) — distinct biomarker signatures at different severity levels. Most other
  biomarkers (TOS, OSI, ARES, asprosin) did not differ significantly between groups,
  which actually strengthens the specificity of the positive findings. The study is
  cross-sectional, so causality cannot be established — does caries cause oxidative
  stress, or does a pro-oxidant state predispose to caries? The sample is small. For
  students: this is mechanistic hypothesis-generation, not practice-changing evidence.
  But it opens a question about whether antioxidant status could serve as a caries-risk
  biomarker in pediatric screening.'
