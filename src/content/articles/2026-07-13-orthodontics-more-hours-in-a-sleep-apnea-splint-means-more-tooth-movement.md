  second-line treatment option in patients with mild to moderate obstructive sleep
  apnea (OSA). Greater MAD adherence is associated with improved treatment success,
  but with increasing wearing time (WT) dental side effects might become more pronounced.
  Thus, objective assessment of WT and sophisticated methods for early detection of
  resulting adverse effects are necessary. Materials and methods In this cross-sectional
  study 29 patients (8 female and 21 male) showing mild to moderate OSA were treated
  with Thornton Adjustable Positioners (TAP-T) modified with an intraoral sensor to
  objectively assess WT. Prior to (T0) and after 12 months of treatment (T1) cephalometric

  closest point algorithm to analyze dental changes within specific subregions in

summary: 'Intraoral sensors objectively measured wearing time (mean 5.52 h/night)
  in 29 OSA patients using mandibular advancement devices over 12 months. 3D scan
  superimposition showed dose-dependent dental side effects: labial lower incisor
  tipping, palatal upper incisor movement, and posterior tooth elongation all scaled
  with hours worn. Skeletal changes were minor.'
summaryDeep: 'Mandibular advancement devices (MADs) are effective for mild-to-moderate
  OSA, but their dental side effects are usually reported as group averages that obscure
  individual variation. This study embedded objective wear sensors in Thornton Adjustable
  Positioners (TAP-T) and combined the data with intraoral scan superimposition (iterative
  closest point algorithm) and cephalometric X-rays at baseline and 12 months. The
  result is a dose-response curve: the more hours worn, the greater the dental displacement,
  particularly in the mandible. Lower incisor labial tipping and posterior tooth elongation
  were the dominant effects. Skeletal changes were minimal at 12 months — reassuring
  for adult patients. The n=29 cross-sectional design limits power, and the TAP-T
  device may not generalise to other MAD designs. But the sensor-plus-3D-scan methodology
  is a model for future MAD trials, and the dose-response finding has immediate counselling
  implications: patients who wear their device more need more frequent dental monitoring.'
