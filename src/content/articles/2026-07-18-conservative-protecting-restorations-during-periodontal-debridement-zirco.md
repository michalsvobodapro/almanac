excerpt: Periodontal debridement using ultrasonic instruments and air polishing is
  a standard procedure in clinical dental practice. While effective in managing periodontal
  health, these techniques can compromise the integrity of dental restorations. This
  article reviews laboratory evidence on the effects of ultrasonic instrumentation
  and air polishing on various restorative biomaterials, focusing on surface roughness
  and marginal integrity. Findings indicate that high-strength ceramics such as zirconia
  and lithium disilicate are more resistant to damage than feldspathic ceramics, resin-modified
  glass-ionomer cements, and resin-based materials, which are more susceptible to
  damage. The use of low-abrasive powders such as erythritol and glycine is recommended
  to mitigate adverse effects. Actionable recommendations are provided to inform favorable
  maintenance protocols for restorations during periodontal therapy.

summaryDeep: 'Periodontal maintenance is a routine procedure, but the interaction
  between debridement instruments and restorative materials is rarely taught systematically.
  This review synthesizes laboratory evidence on surface roughness and marginal integrity
  changes caused by ultrasonic scalers and air polishing across material classes.
  The hierarchy is clear: zirconia and lithium disilicate are highly resistant; feldspathic
  porcelain, RMGIC, and composite are not. Air polishing with sodium bicarbonate causes
  the most surface damage to vulnerable materials; erythritol and glycine powders
  are significantly gentler. The clinical recommendations are actionable: avoid metal
  ultrasonic tips directly on ceramic margins, use plastic or carbon-fibre tips around
  composite and RMGIC, and default to erythritol or glycine for air polishing in patients
  with multiple restorations. This is exactly the kind of chairside knowledge that
  separates competent maintenance from inadvertent iatrogenic damage.'
