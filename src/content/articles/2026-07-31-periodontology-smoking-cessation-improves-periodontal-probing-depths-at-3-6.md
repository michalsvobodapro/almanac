excerpt: 'Aim This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to investigate the association
  between smoking cessation and periodontitis progression by comparing periodontal
  outcomes in patients who quit smoking vs. those who continued smoking. Methods PubMed,
  Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched for prospective studies (published
  up to March 2025) on smoking cessation and periodontitis progression, with ≥3 months''
  follow-up. The primary outcome was probing depth. R Studio 4.1.3 was used for random-effects
  meta-analyses, sensitivity, and subgroup analyses. Results Six studies ( n = 1,934)
  were included. Smoking cessation was associated with short-term improvements in
  PD at 3 months (SMD = −1.08, 95%CI = −1.84 to −0.31, P &amp;lt; 0.01) and 6 months
  (SMD = −1.50, 95%CI = −2.45 to −0.55, P &amp;lt; 0.01), as well as CAL at 3 months
  (SMD = −0.78, 95%CI = −1.39 to −0.16, P = 0.01). However, these associations were
  not sustained at 12 months (PD: P = 0.10; CAL: P = 0.27). No significant associations
  were observed for BOP or PI at any follow-up interval. Conclusion Smoking cessation
  may be associated with modest short-term improvements in periodontal parameters,
  particularly PD and CAL. However, further high-quality studies with longer follow-up
  are warranted to confirm clinical relevance.'

summary: A systematic review and meta-analysis of six prospective studies (n=1,934)
  found that smoking cessation was associated with significant short-term reductions
  in probing depth and clinical attachment loss at 3 and 6 months, but these gains
  were not sustained at 12 months. Bleeding on probing and plaque index showed no
  significant change at any time point. The evidence supports cessation counselling,
  but sets realistic expectations about the timeline of periodontal benefit.
summaryDeep: Six prospective studies with at least three months' follow-up were pooled,
  covering 1,934 patients. Smoking cessation produced a standardised mean difference
  of −1.08 in probing depth at 3 months and −1.50 at 6 months — large effect sizes
  — but the signal disappeared by 12 months (P=0.10). Clinical attachment level improvements
  followed the same pattern. Bleeding on probing and plaque index were unaffected
  throughout. The attenuation at 12 months likely reflects the complexity of periodontal
  healing and the confounding of ongoing professional care in both groups. For clinical
  practice, the data justify integrating smoking cessation support into periodontal
  therapy as a standard of care, with the honest caveat that the periodontal benefit
  may be modest and slow to consolidate. The short-term improvement is still clinically
  meaningful and may motivate patients during the critical early cessation window.
