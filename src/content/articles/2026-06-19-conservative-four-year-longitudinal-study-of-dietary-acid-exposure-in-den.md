summaryDeep: 'This four-year longitudinal study followed 91 dental students annually,
  converting self-reported dietary frequencies into estimated daily acid exposure
  events and tracking diet-related attitudes. The group-level picture was stable —
  no dramatic shifts in acid exposure over time — but individual-level variability
  was substantial, meaning a single dietary assessment may misclassify patients. Critically,
  fruit and juice consumption drove total acid exposure more than soft drinks, which
  challenges the clinical habit of focusing erosion counselling exclusively on carbonated
  beverages. Four baseline intake phenotypes were identified (lower-intake/low-acid,
  fruit-juice, wine-vinegar, soft-drink), with moderate phenotype persistence over
  four years. Attitude items showed low internal consistency, suggesting attitude-based
  screening tools are unreliable. For a student conducting dietary counselling for
  erosive tooth wear, the practical shift is: ask specifically about fruit juice and
  wine frequency, not just fizzy drinks, and consider phenotype-based counselling
  rather than generic advice.'
