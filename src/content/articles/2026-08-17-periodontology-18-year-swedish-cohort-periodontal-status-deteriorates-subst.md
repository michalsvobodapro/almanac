excerpt: 'The prevalence of periodontitis increases with aging. The present study
  aimed to assess descriptively the changes in periodontal status over an 18-year
  period among individuals aged 60 and 66 years at baseline who participated in the
  Swedish National Study on Aging and Care in Karlskrona, Sweden. This longitudinal
  study used quantitative methods based on data from the ongoing project, The Swedish
  National Study on Aging and Care (SNAC) in Karlskrona, Sweden. A total of 340 individuals
  aged 60 and 66 years underwent periodontal and radiographic examinations at baseline
  (2001-2003). Follow-up assessments were conducted after 12 years (2013-2015; n =
  217) and after 18 years (2019-2021; n = 79). A paired t-test, McNemar''s test, and
  binary logistic regression were performed for the statistical analysis. Statistical
  significance was determined at p < 0.05. Overall, the mean value BOP (SD) increased
  from 19.71(17.6) % at baseline to 32.73 (21.7) % at the 18-year follow-up (Cohen''s
  d = ‒0.57; 95%CI: ‒0.81 to ‒0.33; p < 0.001). The proportion of participants with
  at least one site exhibiting a bone level ≥ 5 mm increased significantly, from 49.4%
  at baseline to 88.6% at the 18-year follow-up, corresponding to an absolute increase
  of 39.2 percentage points (p < 0.001). In addition, the prevalence of periodontitis
  increased from 7.8% to 19.5% over the same period, an absolute increase of 11.7
  percentage points (p = 0.035). The present study indicates that periodontal status
  deteriorates'

summary: An 18-year longitudinal study of 340 Swedes (aged 60–66 at baseline, n=79
  at final follow-up) documents a near-doubling of periodontitis prevalence from 7.8%
  to 19.5%, and a jump in sites with bone loss ≥5 mm from 49% to 89%. Bleeding on

summaryDeep: 'The Swedish National Study on Aging and Care (SNAC-Karlskrona) followed
  participants aged 60 and 66 at baseline through 12-year (n=217) and 18-year (n=79)
  periodontal re-examinations. Mean BOP increased from 19.7% to 32.7% (Cohen''s d
  = 0.57), and the proportion with at least one site showing bone level ≥5 mm rose
  from 49.4% to 88.6% — an absolute increase of 39.2 percentage points. Periodontitis
  prevalence climbed from 7.8% to 19.5% (p = 0.035). Attrition is the key limitation:
  the 18-year cohort is 23% of the original sample, and survivors are likely healthier
  than dropouts, meaning these figures probably underestimate true population-level
  deterioration. The study nonetheless provides rare long-term data confirming that
  periodontal aging is not merely a cross-sectional artefact. For clinicians managing
  older patients, this underscores the need for sustained supportive periodontal therapy
  well into the seventh and eighth decades — not a tapering of recall frequency as
  patients age.'
