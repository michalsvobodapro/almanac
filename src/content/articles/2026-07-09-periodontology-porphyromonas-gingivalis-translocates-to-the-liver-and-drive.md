excerpt: Background Multiple factors contribute to the progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated
  steatohepatitis (MASH), which can lead to severe conditions, such as liver cirrhosis
  and hepatocellular carcinoma. Therefore, identifying the risk factors, developing
  targeted treatment strategies, and implementing preventive measures are essential
  to combat this disease. Periodontitis is a highly prevalent infectious disease worldwide,
  and it is a significant risk factor for the progression of systemic diseases, including
  MASH. However, therapeutic strategies for MASH are limited, particularly those that
  address the potential translocation of periodontal pathogens. In this review, the
  contribution of periodontitis to the progression of MASH is explored, and its potential
  for providing new therapeutic insights is discussed. Highlight Recent studies, including
  in animal models and using clinical observations, suggest that Porphyromonas gingivalis
  translocates to the liver and promotes hepatic inflammation and fibrotic progression
  in MASH. In vitro studies have shown that gingipains, which are trypsin-like proteases
  produced by P. gingivalis, proteolytically activate protease-activated receptor
  2 in hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells. Gingipains contribute to fibrosis by
  inducing production of transforming growth factor β, which activates hepatic stellate
  cells. Gingipain inhibitors have the potential to suppress activation of stellate

summaryDeep: 'Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) — the progressive
  form of fatty liver disease — has few therapeutic options and rising global prevalence.
  This review makes the case that periodontitis is a meaningful contributor to MASH
  progression through a specific and mechanistically defined pathway: P. gingivalis
  bacteremia leads to hepatic colonization, where gingipains proteolytically activate
  protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR-2) on hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells.
  This triggers TGF-β production, stellate cell activation, and fibrotic progression.
  In vitro, gingipain inhibitors block this cascade. The evidence base is still largely
  preclinical, but the mechanistic specificity is more compelling than the generic
  ''systemic inflammation'' narrative that has historically linked periodontal and
  systemic disease. For clinicians, the implication is that periodontal treatment
  in MASH patients deserves prospective RCT evaluation as a hepatoprotective intervention
  — a trial that does not yet exist at scale.'
