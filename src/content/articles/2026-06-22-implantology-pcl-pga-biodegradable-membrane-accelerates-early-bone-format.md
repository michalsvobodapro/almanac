excerpt: This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a biodegradable membrane composed
  of polycaprolactone (PCL) and polyglycolic acid (PGA) for guided bone regeneration
  in bone defects. Twelve Wistar albino rats were initially included in the study.
  During follow-up, 3 rats were excluded. To maintain the planned sample size, these
  animals were replaced with 3 additional rats. Consequently, a total of 15 rats were
  used, and the final statistical analyses were based on 12 rats. Twenty-four standardized
  bilateral parietal bone defects were created and randomly allocated to four groups.
  Defects received either 3D-printed PCL membranes or PCL membranes coated on the
  inner surface with PGA (PCL/PGA). Control defects received PCL membranes alone.
  Group 1 received PCL/PGA with a human-derived freeze-dried bone graft, an equine
  xenograft, and autologous blood. Group 2 received PCL/PGA with autologous blood.
  Group 3 received PCL with autologous blood. Six rats were euthanized at 4 weeks
  and six at 8 weeks. Histopathological and stereological analyses were performed
  to assess newly formed bone volume. At week 4, Group 2 showed significantly greater
  newly formed bone volume and total tissue volume than all other groups ( p < 0.001).
  At week 8, newly formed bone volume in Group 2 remained significantly greater than
  in Groups 1 and 3 ( p < 0.001) but did not differ from the control group ( p > 0.05).
  In this GBR model, the PGA-coated PCL membrane enhanced new bone formation during
  the early phase

summary: In a rat calvarial defect model, a PGA-coated PCL membrane outperformed plain
  PCL at 4 weeks for new bone volume, though the advantage narrowed by 8 weeks. The
  PGA coating appears to enhance early osteogenesis, likely via improved cell adhesion
  and degradation kinetics. Animal data only — clinical translation is distant.
summaryDeep: 'Biodegradable membranes for GBR avoid the second surgery required for
  non-resorbable options, but controlling degradation rate to match bone formation
  has been the persistent challenge. This study tested a 3D-printed PCL membrane with
  an inner PGA coating against plain PCL in standardized rat parietal bone defects,
  with and without bone graft. At 4 weeks, the PCL/PGA group with autologous blood
  (no graft) showed significantly greater new bone volume than all other groups. By
  8 weeks, the advantage over the control narrowed and was no longer significant versus
  the plain PCL group — suggesting the PGA coating accelerates early healing rather
  than improving final bone volume. The graft-containing group paradoxically underperformed,
  possibly due to space competition or inflammatory response. Limitations are substantial:
  n=12 rats, no human data, and the 8-week endpoint is short for bone maturation assessment.
  This is basic science informing future membrane design, not a clinical recommendation.'
