excerpt: To examine differences in the occurrence of consequences of untreated dental
  caries (pulpal involvement, ulceration, fistula, and abscess-PUFA) across intersectional
  social positions defined by sex, skin color, and household income among adolescents.
  This study analyzed data from the Brazilian National Oral Health Survey (SB Brasil
  2023), using a multi-stage stratified probability sampling design to represent adolescents
  aged 15-19 years. Social variables were combined into eight intersectional groups
  based on sex, skin color, and family income. Outcomes included PUFA prevalence ≥1
  and counts of its components. Survey-adjusted Poisson regression with robust variance
  and a three-way interaction term was applied. The sample comprised 8,041 adolescents.
  Compared to high-income White males, prevalence was higher among low-income Black
  males (PR=5.93; 95%CI:2.60-13.51) and low-income Black females (PR=4.06; 95%CI:1.85-8.90).
  The predicted prevalence of PUFA ≥1 ranged from 4.0% (95%CI:2.0-7.0) among high-income

summaryDeep: 'PUFA indices capture the clinical consequences of untreated caries that
  go beyond cavitation — pulpal involvement, ulceration, fistula, and abscess — and
  are increasingly used in epidemiological surveys as markers of treatment need severity.
  This study applies an intersectional analysis to the SB Brasil 2023 national survey,
  combining sex, skin color, and household income into eight social position groups
  and modeling PUFA prevalence with survey-adjusted Poisson regression. The gradient
  is stark: low-income Black males face a prevalence ratio of 5.93 compared to high-income
  White males. Females show a different pattern — high-income Black females had unexpectedly
  high prevalence, suggesting that skin color interacts with income differently by
  sex. The intersectional approach reveals heterogeneity that single-variable analyses
  would miss. For a Prague student, the Brazilian context is distant, but the methodological
  lesson — that social determinants of oral health cannot be disaggregated into independent
  variables — is directly relevant to understanding oral health inequalities in the
  Czech Republic and EU.'
