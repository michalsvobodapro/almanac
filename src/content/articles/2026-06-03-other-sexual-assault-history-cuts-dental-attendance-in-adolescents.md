  were significantly less likely to use dental services, and dental pain amplified
  rather than corrected that gap. The finding reframes dental avoidance as a trauma-linked
  behaviour, not just a cost or access problem. Clinicians screening adolescent no-shows
  should consider adverse childhood experiences as a structural barrier.
summaryDeep: 'Using 2019 national Brazilian school-health survey data on 13–17-year-olds,
  this cross-sectional study tested whether self-reported sexual assault predicted
  lower dental service utilisation — and whether dental pain moderated that relationship.
  It did, in the wrong direction: pain did not drive assault-affected adolescents
  toward care; instead, the combination of trauma history and active pain was associated
  with even greater avoidance. Sex differences were also examined, though the abstract
  does not detail the direction. The clinical implication is uncomfortable but clear:
  the standard assumption that pain motivates help-seeking breaks down in trauma-exposed
  populations. For a dental student in a clinical rotation, this is a reminder that
  a teenager who presents late with advanced caries or untreated pulpitis may be navigating
  something far beyond appointment logistics. Trauma-informed intake screening — already
  standard in some EU paediatric dental guidelines — gains another evidence brick
  here.'
