summary: A Siberian Neanderthal molar bears tool marks consistent with deliberate
  caries excavation — pushing the earliest known invasive dental intervention back
  to 59,000 years ago, tens of thousands of years before Homo sapiens left comparable
  evidence. The finding reframes dental care not as a civilisational invention but
  as a deep cognitive behaviour shared across hominins.
summaryDeep: 'Researchers analysing a 59,000-year-old Neanderthal molar from Siberia
  have identified micro-striations and cavity morphology consistent with intentional
  mechanical removal of carious tissue using a lithic tool — making it the oldest
  documented example of operative dentistry by a wide margin. Previous record-holders
  were Homo sapiens specimens from roughly 14,000 years ago. The significance isn''t
  merely chronological: it implies that the cognitive leap required to connect tooth
  pain, decay, and the idea of excavation predates modern human anatomy. For dental
  students, it''s a useful corrective to the assumption that evidence-based thinking
  in dentistry is recent — the instinct to intervene is apparently ancient. The study
  also raises questions about pain management and social cooperation: someone had
  to hold still, and someone else had to know what they were doing.'
