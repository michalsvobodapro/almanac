  issues. Vitamins C and D may influence gingival health through collagen metabolism
  and immune regulation. The study aimed to assess whether vitamin C and D supplementation
  can reduce bleeding on probing (BOP), measured as the percentage of bleeding sites
  (BOP%). A total of 136 school-going children, aged 8-16 years old, were included
  in this randomized controlled trial (RCT). Eligible participants presenting to the
  University of Lahore Dental Hospital were recruited. Oral health status was determined
  using the BOP examination. Participants were divided into two groups: Group A (standard
  care) and Group B (standard care plus vitamin C and D supplements). Children in
  both groups A and B were treated with ultrasonic scaling and given structured oral
  hygiene instructions. Children in group B (intervention group) were given an 8-week
  plan of vitamin C and D supplementation. Participants were recalled at 4 and 8 weeks
  to reassess. Among 150 enrolled children, 14 were lost to follow-up; 136 were included
  in analysis (Group A n = 68, Group B n = 68). Baseline BOP% was significantly higher

summary: An 8-week RCT in 136 school-age children found that adding vitamin C and
  D supplements to ultrasonic scaling and oral hygiene instruction reduced bleeding

summaryDeep: 'One hundred thirty-six children aged 8–16 were randomized to standard
  care (scaling plus oral hygiene instruction) or standard care plus 8 weeks of vitamin
  C and D supplementation. Bleeding on probing percentage was the primary outcome,
  assessed at baseline, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks. The supplementation group showed significantly
  lower BOP% at both follow-up points, even after adjusting for a higher baseline
  BOP in that group. The biological rationale is plausible: vitamin C supports collagen
  synthesis and gingival tissue integrity, while vitamin D modulates immune and inflammatory
  pathways relevant to gingival inflammation. However, the 8-week window is short,
  the study is single-center (Lahore), and the dose and formulation of supplements
  are not detailed in the excerpt — all factors that limit direct clinical adoption.
  This is promising adjunctive evidence, not a protocol change, particularly for patients
  with nutritional deficiencies.'
