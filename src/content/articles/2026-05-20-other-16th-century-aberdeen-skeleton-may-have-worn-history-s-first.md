summary: Archaeologists excavating St Nicholas Kirk in Aberdeen have identified a
  gold ligature on the teeth of a 16th-century Scottish man — potentially the earliest
  documented example of decorative metallic dental jewellery. The ligature spans the
  right lateral and left central incisors and shows no therapeutic function. Humans,
  it turns out, have always wanted gold in their mouths.
summaryDeep: 'The find comes from ongoing excavations at St Nicholas Kirk in Aberdeen,
  where skeletal remains have been dated to approximately the 16th century. A gold
  wire ligature — fixed to the right lateral incisor and left central incisor — shows
  no evidence of therapeutic intent: there is no associated pathology, no splinting
  indication, and no prosthetic function. The researchers propose it is purely decorative,
  making it the earliest known example of elective dental metallic ornamentation.
  The historical context is interesting: 16th-century Scotland had access to gold
  through trade, and dental modification for status display is documented across many
  contemporaneous cultures. For dentistry students, this is a useful reminder that
  the boundary between therapeutic and aesthetic dentistry has always been culturally
  constructed — and that patient demand for dental aesthetics is not a modern invention.'
