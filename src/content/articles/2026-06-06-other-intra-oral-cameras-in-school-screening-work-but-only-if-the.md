excerptFull: Aims This qualitative study explored the feasibility and acceptability
  of incorporating an intra-oral camera into a supervised toothbrushing programme
  by examining the practical factors shaping its use from the perspectives of staff
  involved in its implementation. Methods Six university staff involved in delivering
  the programme were interviewed, and the data were analysed thematically. Results
  Participants reported that the camera improved accuracy, supported clearer communication
  with children. Children engaged well with the technology, and the visual element
  helped make dental screening feel more understandable and approachable. At the same
  time, implementation exposed practical hurdles. Technical setup, data security,
  staffing, and inconsistent operator experience contributed to delays. The process
  was most effective when qualified staff captured the images, highlighting the importance
  of defined roles. Strong teamwork and clear protocols were seen as essential for
  future scaling. Conclusions The findings show that the intra-oral camera is potentially
  valuable within school-based prevention, but its success depends less on the device
  itself and more on the systems built around it. Reliable digital infrastructure,
  dedicated staff time, and a standardised imaging pathway are critical for sustainability.
  With these elements in place, the technology could strengthen early detection and
  enhance the educational aims of supervised toothbrushing programmes.

summaryDeep: 'Six university staff involved in delivering a supervised toothbrushing
  and screening programme were interviewed about their experience integrating intra-oral
  cameras into the workflow. Thematic analysis revealed genuine clinical benefits:
  improved image accuracy over visual inspection alone, better communication with
  children (who engaged positively with seeing their own teeth), and enhanced early
  detection potential. But implementation was repeatedly disrupted by practical failures
  — technical setup complexity, data governance concerns, variable operator competence,
  and undefined staff roles. The study''s key finding is that outcomes were best when
  qualified dental staff captured images, not support workers, pointing to a skills
  and role-definition gap that no amount of better hardware will fix. For students
  interested in public health dentistry or community outreach, this is a realistic
  picture of what technology adoption looks like at the coalface: the device is the
  easy part. Sustainable digital screening programmes require standardised imaging
  protocols, dedicated staff time, and robust data infrastructure — none of which
  come in the box.'
