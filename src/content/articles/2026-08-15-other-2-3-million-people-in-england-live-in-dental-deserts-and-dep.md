excerptFull: 'Aims The aim of this study was to identify high-need dental deserts
  in England and quantify the population affected and profile the demographics relative
  to other deserts. Methods Lower Layer Super Output Area (LSOA) December 2021 boundaries
  were linked to Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) 2025 (deciles; mid-2022 population).
  National Health Service (NHS) dental practices were geocoded using the ONS Postcode
  Directory (British National Grid). Euclidean distance from each LSOA centroid to
  the nearest NHS-listed practice was calculated; LSOAs with no provider within 5
  km were classified as dental deserts. High-need deserts were deserts in IMD deciles
  1–3. Demographic profiling used Census 2021 ethnicity (TS021) and age structure
  (TS007a). Results Across England, 1,340 LSOAs (population 2,342,985) had no NHS-listed
  dental provider within 5 km. The high-need deserts comprised 112 LSOAs (189,542
  residents). A further 2,153,443 residents in the IMD 4–10 group lived in dental
  deserts. The high-need desert population was predominantly white (97.9%) and had
  a similar age structure to other deserts not in the IMD 1–3 group (0–15: 19.4% versus
  19.5%; 65+: 27.4% versus 26.9%). Conclusion Geographic exclusion from NHS-listed
  dental provision affects ~2.3 million residents. Spatial audits linking deprivation
  and access could support targeted service planning.'

summary: A national spatial analysis maps 1,340 lower-layer super output areas in
  England with no NHS dental practice within 5 km, affecting 2.3 million residents.
  The 112 highest-need desert areas — combining geographic exclusion with top-three
  deprivation deciles — house nearly 190,000 people, predominantly in rural and coastal
  communities.
summaryDeep: Using geocoded NHS practice locations, 2021 census boundaries, and the
  Index of Multiple Deprivation 2025, this study calculated Euclidean distance from
  every LSOA centroid to the nearest NHS-listed dental practice. The 5-km threshold
  for 'dental desert' is conservative — it assumes car access and ignores appointment
  availability — meaning the true access gap is likely larger. The high-need desert
  population (IMD deciles 1–3 plus no provider within 5 km) was 97.9% white and had
  an older age structure, pointing to rural and coastal deprivation rather than urban
  ethnic-minority communities as the primary geography of exclusion. The methodology
  — spatial audit linked to deprivation indices — is directly transferable to Czech
  or EU-level access mapping, making this a useful methodological reference for anyone
  interested in oral health equity research. The moderate evidence grade reflects
  the robustness of the spatial analysis, though it cannot capture appointment availability
  or actual utilisation.
