  with differences in cultivable microbial diversity in clinically healthy peri-implant
  tissues. Methods fifty-two patients with single molar implants were included after
  a standardized 3-month healing period and stratified into three groups according
  to implant mucosal tunnel depth: &lt;3 mm, 3–5 mm, and &gt; 5 mm. Microbial samples
  from the inner lining of the implant mucosal tunnel were cultured under aerobic
  and anaerobic conditions and identified using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. As a
  secondary exploratory outcome, TNF-α expression in peri-implant mucosa was quantified
  using real-time PCR. Results a total of 262 cultivable isolates representing 46
  species were identified, with facultative anaerobic species predominating (81.68%).

summaryDeep: 'Fifty-two patients with single molar implants were stratified by mucosal
  tunnel depth (<3 mm, 3–5 mm, >5 mm) after a standardized 3-month healing period.
  MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry identified 262 cultivable isolates across 46 species;
  facultative anaerobes predominated (81.68%). The 3–5 mm group showed the highest
  Shannon diversity index, while tunnels >5 mm showed reduced diversity with streptococci
  dominating — a shift toward a less complex, potentially more pathogenic community.
  TNF-α expression in peri-implant mucosa increased progressively with depth, reaching
  approximately 38-fold higher levels in the >5 mm group (p <0.017). The study is
  exploratory and cross-sectional, so causality cannot be established, and cultivable
  microbiota underestimates total microbial diversity. Still, the data raise a practical
  question: should implant mucosal tunnel depth be documented and monitored as a clinical
  parameter alongside probing depth and bleeding on probing? For students learning
  implant maintenance, this is a reminder that ''clinically healthy'' tissue can harbour
  a significantly altered inflammatory milieu.'
