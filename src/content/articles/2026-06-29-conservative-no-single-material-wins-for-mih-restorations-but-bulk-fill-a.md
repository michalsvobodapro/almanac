  enamel that compromises tooth structure, impairing the adhesion of restorative materials
  and reducing restoration longevity. In this context, the present study aimed to
  analyze, through an integrative literature review, the clinical performance of the
  main restorative materials used in the treatment of teeth affected by MIH. The search
  was conducted in the PubMed, Embase, and Virtual Health Library (BVS) databases,
  using descriptors related to MIH and restorative materials combined with Boolean
  operators. Studies published in English and Portuguese, available in full text,
  and addressing the clinical performance of these materials were included. After
  the selection process, 10 studies were included in the review. The results showed

summaryDeep: 'Molar-incisor hypomineralization (MIH) creates a hostile substrate for
  adhesive dentistry: the hypomineralized enamel is porous, protein-contaminated,
  and mechanically weak, compromising bond strength for conventional composites. This
  integrative review (10 studies, PubMed/Embase/BVS) maps the clinical performance
  landscape across material classes. Conventional composites fail disproportionately
  in severe MIH, particularly at enamel margins. Glass ionomers tolerate the substrate
  better via chemical adhesion and provide fluoride release, but their lower fracture
  toughness limits use in high-stress zones. Bulk-fill resins emerge as the best-performing
  direct option in several studies, with improved marginal adaptation and reduced
  polymerisation stress. For severely affected first molars, stainless steel or zirconia
  crowns offer the greatest clinical predictability. The review is limited by the
  small number of included studies and heterogeneous outcome measures. For the student
  clinician, the practical algorithm is: mild-moderate MIH → bulk-fill composite with
  careful adhesive protocol; severe MIH with structural loss → crown.'
