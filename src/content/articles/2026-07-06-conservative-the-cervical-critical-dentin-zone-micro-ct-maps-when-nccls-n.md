  extracted human premolars were analyzed using micro-computed tomography. Lesion
  depth, lesion height, depth/height ratio, CCD height, and RDT were measured using
  standardized cross-sectional images. Linear regression assessed the association

  the RDT and lesion depth. Diagnostic performance for detecting critical RDT was

summaryDeep: 'Non-carious cervical lesions (NCCLs) present a perennial clinical dilemma:
  when is the lesion deep enough to justify restoration? This micro-CT study of 139
  extracted premolar NCCLs defines the ''critical cervical dentin'' (CCD) — the zone
  from the cervical enamel limit to the occlusal limit of tertiary dentin — as the
  anatomical hotspot for dangerously thin remaining dentin. Every 1 mm increase in
  lesion depth reduced RDT by 0.637 mm. CCD inner-half involvement and a probing depth
  ≥1.5 mm together showed good diagnostic performance for detecting RDT ≤1.0 mm, but
  only when lesion height was ≤2.5 mm; taller lesions showed variable performance
  across tomographic profiles. The study is in-vitro and cannot account for pulp response
  or patient-specific factors, but it provides a rational anatomical framework for
  the restoration decision that goes beyond simple depth thresholds. For students,
  this is a useful conceptual anchor: probe the lesion, estimate height, and think
  about CCD involvement before defaulting to ''monitor and wait.'''
