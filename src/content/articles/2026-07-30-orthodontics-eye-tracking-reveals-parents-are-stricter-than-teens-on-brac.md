excerpt: Abstract The objective of this study was to analyze visual perception of
  patients undergoing orthodontic treatment with multi-bracket appliances (MBA), and
  examine differences in oral hygiene perception among adolescents, their guardians,
  and dental students. The lay person group included 56 participants (28 adolescents
  and guardians), while the expert group consisted of 30 dentistry students. Both
  groups evaluated randomized intraoral frontal photographs of adolescents with MBA,
  taken with or without a plaque disclosing agent. Using eye-tracking methods, eye
  movements were recorded during the evaluation. Significant differences in visual
  path length and oral hygiene evaluation were found. The expert group evaluated oral
  hygiene most accurately, with parents being stricter and adolescents more lenient.
  The expert group’s visual path was significantly shorter than the lay person group’s.
  Use of a plaque disclosing agent improved the accuracy of oral hygiene evaluation
  ( p &lt; 0.001). A correlation was observed between oral hygiene behavior and evaluation.
  In conclusion, dental students provided the most accurate oral hygiene evaluations.
  Parents were stricter, and adolescents were more lenient. Among adolescents, longer
  brushing time was weakly associated with more accurate evaluations, and plaque disclosing
  agents enhanced evaluation accuracy.

summaryDeep: 'Orthodontic patients in multi-bracket appliances are at elevated caries
  and gingivitis risk, yet compliance with oral hygiene is notoriously poor — partly
  because patients and parents misjudge the actual hygiene status. This eye-tracking
  study quantifies those misperceptions: dental students (n=30) showed the shortest
  visual scan paths and highest accuracy; parents were systematically stricter than
  reality warranted; adolescents were more lenient. The use of a plaque disclosing
  agent improved evaluation accuracy significantly (p<0.001) across all groups, and
  among adolescents, longer brushing time correlated weakly but positively with more
  accurate self-assessment. The study is cross-sectional and uses photographs rather
  than live patients, limiting ecological validity. Still, the finding that disclosing
  agents improve lay perception accuracy is directly actionable: incorporating disclosing
  tablets into orthodontic recall appointments gives patients and parents a calibrated
  view of their hygiene status, which may improve motivation. For dental students,
  the data confirm that clinical training genuinely sharpens visual diagnostic efficiency.'
