summary: 'The 2018 AAP/EFP staging-and-grading classification has been widely adopted
  in research and clinical settings, but its validation has focused on clinician utility,
  not patient comprehension. This commentary identifies a critical gap: patients receiving
  a ''Stage III Grade B'' diagnosis have no intuitive framework for what that means
  for their prognosis or behavior change.'
summaryDeep: The 2018 classification was designed to improve clinical communication
  and research standardization — and it has succeeded on both counts. But the authors
  point out that the patient-facing dimension was never formally validated. Terms
  like 'Stage' and 'Grade' carry intuitive meaning in oncology (where patients have
  been educated over decades) but not in periodontology. A patient told they have
  'Stage III Grade B generalized periodontitis' is unlikely to understand severity,
  urgency, or the behavioral implications without additional translation. The commentary
  calls for patient-facing communication tools — visual aids, plain-language equivalents
  — to be developed and tested alongside the classification itself. This is not a
  critique of the classification's scientific validity, which is well-established.
  It is a call to close the loop between clinical taxonomy and patient activation,
  which is ultimately what drives compliance with supportive periodontal therapy.
  For a student, this is a reminder that diagnosis delivery is a communication skill,
  not just a classification exercise.
