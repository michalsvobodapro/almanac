summaryDeep: Tooth loss alters oropharyngeal anatomy — reduced vertical dimension,
  tongue posture changes, and loss of posterior support can all compromise upper airway
  patency during sleep. This cross-sectional study (Chilean National Health Survey
  2016–17, n=5,009) tested whether tooth loss categories predicted four sleep quality
  indicators and sleep duration. Edentulism remained independently associated with
  snoring (OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.29–2.20) and daytime sleepiness (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.15–2.17)
  after adjustment for age, BMI, smoking, and comorbidities. Severe tooth loss (1–9
  teeth) showed similar, if attenuated, associations. The excessive sleep duration
  finding did not survive full adjustment, pointing to residual confounding by systemic
  disease burden. Cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. For clinical
  practice, the data add to a growing evidence base linking oral rehabilitation —
  particularly complete denture or implant-supported prosthetic restoration — to sleep
  health outcomes. Screening edentulous patients for sleep-disordered breathing is
  a low-cost, high-yield clinical habit.
