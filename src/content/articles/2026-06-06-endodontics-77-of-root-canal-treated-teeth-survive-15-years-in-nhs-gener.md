excerptFull: Aims To investigate the 15-year survival of teeth that had root canal
  treatment (RCT) in adult patients treated within the NHS General Dental Services
  (NHS GDS), and to further examine dentist and patient factors affecting outcomes.
  Methods The source of data was an age-stratified random sample of NHS GDS patients
  and RCT they received from 1990–2006. The survival was measured to reintervention
  after RCT, using modified Kaplan-Meier methodology and investigating dentist and
  patient factors. Results The 15-year survival of 791,375 root canal-treated teeth
  was 77% based on reintervention (retreatment, surgery, extraction) and 83% based
  on extraction alone. The investigation of general factors that affect survival of
  root canal-treated teeth indicated that there were two dentist (age and location
  of the dental practice) and two patient (age and charge-paying status) factors of
  relevance. Among tooth-specific factors, lower teeth, posterior teeth and molar
  teeth performed better than their counterparts. Conclusions The 15-year survival
  of root canal treated teeth in this study was encouraging and supports the provision
  of root canal treatment by general dental practitioners working in the NHS GDS.
  It was most affected by geographical region of the dental practice, age of the patient
  and the location of the tooth in the mouth.

summary: Analysis of nearly 800,000 NHS root canal treatments shows 77% survival at
  15 years when measured by reintervention, rising to 83% when measured by extraction
  alone. Mandibular and posterior teeth outperform anterior and maxillary counterparts.
  Patient age, geographic region, and charge-paying status — a proxy for socioeconomic
  status — are the dominant modifying factors.
summaryDeep: 'This is the largest long-term RCT survival dataset ever published: 791,375
  teeth treated between 1990 and 2006 in NHS General Dental Services, followed using
  modified Kaplan-Meier methodology. The 77% reintervention-free survival and 83%
  extraction-free survival at 15 years are robust benchmarks for general practice
  — not specialist — settings, which makes them directly relevant to what students
  will encounter in the clinic. Lower, posterior, and molar teeth consistently outperformed
  upper anterior teeth, likely reflecting differences in root anatomy, occlusal loading,
  and restorability. Two dentist-level factors mattered: practitioner age and practice
  location (region). Two patient-level factors mattered: patient age and charge-paying
  status. The regional variation is striking and probably reflects differences in
  referral culture, material access, and case complexity thresholds. For consent conversations,
  these numbers are your baseline: a well-treated molar in a middle-aged patient has
  better than 4-in-5 odds of still being present at 15 years.'
