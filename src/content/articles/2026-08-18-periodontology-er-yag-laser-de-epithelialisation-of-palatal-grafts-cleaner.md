excerpt: The aim of this case series was to introduce intraoral Er:YAG laser-assisted
  de-epithelialization of autogenous gingival graft (EL-DGG) for root coverage and
  alveolar ridge augmentation. In conventional harvesting of de-epithelialized gingival
  grafts (DGGs) with a scalpel, the presence of residual epithelium has occasionally
  been observed, posing challenges to the reliable procurement of stable connective
  tissue grafts (SCTG). We utilized an Er:YAG dental laser, known for its minimal
  histological effect among dental lasers, to perform intraoral DGG in three cases
  involving root coverage or ridge augmentation. Complete root coverage was achieved
  in the two root coverage cases. In the ridge augmentation case, horizontal and vertical
  dimensional gains of 1.56 and 0.74 mm, respectively, were observed. A portion of
  the CTG obtained from the peripheral trimmed area was biopsied and histologically
  examined. De-epithelialization of this area was performed using the same protocol
  as in the clinical procedure, including both Er:YAG laser irradiation and adjunctive
  rotary brush application, suggesting that epithelial removal was achieved in the
  examined sample with minimal damage to the underlying connective tissue layer of
  dense collagen fibers. By effectively eliminating epithelial remnants and preserving
  connective tissue thickness in palatal grafts, EL-DGG can offer more favorable clinical
  outcomes in root coverage and ridge augmentation compared to conventional graft-harvesting

summaryDeep: Residual epithelium on connective tissue grafts is a known complication
  of conventional scalpel harvesting that can compromise graft integration and clinical
  outcomes. This case series used an Er:YAG laser — chosen for its minimal thermal
  side-effect profile among dental lasers — combined with adjunctive rotary brush
  application to de-epithelialise palatal grafts in three patients undergoing root
  coverage or ridge augmentation. Histological biopsy of the peripheral trimmed area
  confirmed complete epithelial removal with preservation of the dense collagen fibre
  architecture of the underlying connective tissue. Clinical outcomes were positive
  in all three cases. The evidence level is low — three cases, no controls, no long-term
  follow-up — and this technique requires laser equipment and operator training that
  many practices lack. It is a technically interesting approach that warrants a proper
  RCT before it can be recommended over conventional harvesting.
