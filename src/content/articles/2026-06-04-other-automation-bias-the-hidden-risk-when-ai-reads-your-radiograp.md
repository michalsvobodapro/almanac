summary: AI diagnostic tools in dentistry are proliferating faster than the evidence
  for safe clinical integration. This BDJ piece names automation bias — the tendency
  to defer to algorithmic output even when clinical judgment should override it —
  as a growing patient-safety concern. It's a timely corrective for a profession being
  sold AI as a liability shield rather than a decision-support tool.
summaryDeep: 'Automation bias is well-documented in aviation and radiology, and dentistry
  is now squarely in its path. As AI-assisted caries detection, periapical lesion
  identification, and bone-level measurement tools enter everyday practice, clinicians
  risk anchoring on algorithmic outputs rather than integrating them critically with
  clinical findings. The BDJ commentary argues that the very confidence AI tools project
  — clean overlays, percentage scores, colour-coded findings — is precisely what makes
  them dangerous when they err. The risk is compounded in dental education: students
  trained alongside AI may never fully develop the independent pattern-recognition
  skills needed to catch AI errors. The piece calls for structured training in AI
  limitations as a core competency, not an afterthought. For a student in Prague entering
  a profession where AI tools are increasingly embedded in practice management software,
  understanding when not to trust the algorithm is as important as knowing how to
  use it.'
