  unclear. This study characterized age-related oral microbiome alterations, developed

summaryDeep: 'Using 16S rRNA sequencing data from 4,763 NHANES participants aged 14
  and older, this cross-sectional study asks whether age drives caries accumulation
  directly or partly through microbiome remodelling. The answer is both: age accounts
  for 40.5% of DMFS variance in a non-linear positive curve, but mediation analysis
  shows the microbiome is a significant intermediary — not a bystander. Older adults
  show reduced microbial diversity, altered taxonomic composition, and disrupted co-occurrence
  networks, all of which associate with higher caries burden. A random-forest-derived
  Oral Microbial Health Index (OMHI) integrates these signals into a single score
  with potential clinical utility for risk stratification. The cross-sectional design
  limits causal inference, and the OMHI needs prospective validation before it touches
  a patient chart. Still, for a Prague dental student thinking about prevention in
  older adult patients, this reframes the conversation: caries risk in a 65-year-old
  isn''t just about diet and fluoride — the microbial ecology has shifted in ways
  that may require age-tailored protocols.'
