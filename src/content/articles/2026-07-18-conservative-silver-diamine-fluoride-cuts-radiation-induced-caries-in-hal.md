excerpt: Radiation-induced caries (RIC) is a major complication of head and neck cancer
  radiotherapy. This study evaluated the effectiveness of professionally applied 38%
  silver diamine fluoride (SDF) in preventing coronal and root RIC. In this randomized,
  single-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 56 patients were assigned to receive topical
  38% SDF or placebo before radiotherapy and at 1 and 3 months after radiotherapy.
  The primary outcome was caries increment, measured as the number of new decayed,
  missing (due to caries), or filled tooth surfaces at 1, 3, and 6 months. Secondary
  outcomes were stimulated salivary flow rate, buffering capacity, gingival index,
  and oral health-related quality of life. Thirty-six participants completed at least
  1 follow-up visit (18 SDF, 18 placebo). Mean (SD) participant age was 61 (13) years,
  and most were men (78.0%). Median caries increment was significantly lower in the
  SDF group at 3 months (1.0 vs 2.0; P = .027) and 6 months (2.0 vs 3.0; P = .030).
  At 3 months, 47.1% of SDF participants developed no new lesions compared with 5.9%
  of those in the placebo group (P = .017). Stimulated salivary flow decreased significantly
  in both groups, with no between-group differences. No significant differences were
  observed in gingival inflammation or quality of life measures. Topical 38% SDF reduced
  RIC progression despite profound hyposalivation, supporting its use as a practical

summaryDeep: Radiation-induced caries is one of the most destructive and underappreciated
  sequelae of head-and-neck radiotherapy, driven by salivary gland destruction and
  direct enamel damage. This RCT randomized 56 patients to 38% SDF or placebo at three
  time points bracketing radiotherapy; 36 completed at least one follow-up. Median
  caries increment was lower in the SDF arm at both 3 months (1.0 vs 2.0, P=.027)
  and 6 months (2.0 vs 3.0, P=.030), and the proportion with zero new lesions at 3
  months was 47% vs 6% (P=.017). Salivary flow declined equally in both groups, confirming
  SDF's protective effect is independent of saliva. No differences in gingival inflammation
  or quality of life were detected. The study is small and single-blind, but the effect
  size is clinically meaningful and the intervention is cheap and chairside-simple.
  For oncology patients heading into RT, SDF application should be part of the pre-treatment
  dental clearance protocol.
