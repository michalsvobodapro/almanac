summary: 'Complete tooth loss has declined over decades, but a new epidemiological
  analysis asks whether that trend is driven by age, calendar period, or the cohort
  you were born into. Disentangling these effects matters: if cohort is the dominant
  driver, today''s middle-aged adults may age into far lower edentulism rates than
  current projections assume — reshaping implant and denture demand planning.'
summaryDeep: 'Population-level edentulism data are routinely interpreted as a simple
  time trend — fewer people are losing all their teeth, full stop. This study applies
  age-period-cohort modelling to test whether the decline is uniform or whether birth
  cohort membership creates differential trajectories. The distinction is clinically
  and economically consequential: a pure period effect (better dentistry across the
  board) predicts gradual continued decline, while a strong cohort effect means the
  steepest improvements are concentrated in specific generations and may plateau.
  For a student thinking about the future patient mix, this analysis is a useful corrective
  to the assumption that complete denture prosthodontics is simply fading away on
  a smooth curve. It also has implications for public health resource allocation and
  dental school curriculum weighting between fixed and removable prosthodontics.'
