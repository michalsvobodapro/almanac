excerpt: Clear aligner therapy (CAT) is widely used in adult orthodontic treatment,
  but prolonged treatment duration remains a major limitation. Photobiomodulation
  therapy (PBMT) has been proposed as a noninvasive adjunct to accelerate tooth movement
  by modulating cellular activity and bone remodeling. This narrative review summarizes
  the literature on PBMT in orthodontic tooth movement, with particular attention
  to its application in CAT, effects on treatment efficiency, and safety considerations.
  Published clinical studies generally suggest that PBMT may be associated with faster
  alignment and shorter treatment duration. In one historical prospective study of
  170 Invisalign patients, the crowding resolution rate was 0.33 mm/week in the PBMT
  group versus 0.21 mm/week in controls, with a 57.5-week reduction in treatment time.
  A retrospective study of 84 clear aligner patients found mean treatment durations
  of 528 ± 323 days with PBMT compared with 719 ± 220 days in controls, representing
  a 26.6% reduction. The reviewed literature also suggests that wavelengths in the
  780 to 830 nm range are commonly used and that PBMT may increase tooth movement
  speed by approximately 24%, although findings remain heterogeneous across protocols.
  CBCT-based studies found no statistically significant difference in root volume
  change between PBMT and control groups. Overall, PBMT appears promising as an adjunct
  for accelerating tooth movement during CAT, particularly in adult patients treated

summaryDeep: 'Clear aligner therapy''s Achilles heel is time: patients disengage,
  compliance drops, and outcomes drift. PBMT — low-level laser or LED irradiation
  at 780–830 nm — has been proposed as a non-invasive adjunct that upregulates bone
  remodeling and accelerates tooth movement. This narrative review synthesizes the
  available clinical literature, including a prospective study of 170 Invisalign patients
  (crowding resolution 0.33 mm/week PBMT vs. 0.21 mm/week control) and a retrospective
  study of 84 patients (528 vs. 719 days mean treatment duration). The ~24% speed
  increase is consistent across studies, but protocols vary enormously in wavelength,
  power density, and application frequency, making direct comparison difficult. The
  safety signal from CBCT-based root volume studies is reassuring. The narrative review
  design and heterogeneous protocols mean this cannot yet be translated into a specific
  clinical recommendation. For a student, the key takeaway is that PBMT is biologically
  plausible, clinically promising, and commercially available — but the field needs
  standardized RCTs before it earns a place in routine aligner protocols.'
